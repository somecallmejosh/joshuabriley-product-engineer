# Interview with Sasha, Office Manager at IVFCRYO, LLC

**Research focus:** Understanding the administrative and communication challenges involved in safely coordinating reproductive specimen shipments between cryobank facilities

**Josh:** Thanks for speaking with me, Sasha. Can you start by explaining your role at IVFCRYO?

**Sasha:** As office manager, I help keep the administrative side of the operation moving. That includes coordinating with patients, fertility clinics, cryobanks, couriers, and our internal team.

A shipment may involve a relatively short drive between two facilities, but a lot has to happen before the courier can leave. We need accurate customer information, approval from both facilities, the correct shipping unit, a courier schedule, payment confirmation, contact information, and a plan for keeping everyone updated.

My job is often to make sure the right person has the right information at the right time.

**Josh:** At what point do you become involved in a shipment?

**Sasha:** We become involved as soon as the patient places an order or contacts us about moving their specimens.

A customer experience agent is assigned to the case. IVFCRYO describes this person as the patient’s primary contact throughout the process. Our team then works directly with the originating and receiving clinics so the patient does not have to act as the project manager for the transfer.

That first stage is mostly about collecting information and understanding what the shipment requires.

**Josh:** What information do you need from the patient?

**Sasha:** We need to know what type of reproductive material is being moved, where it is currently stored, where it is going, and who the patient has been communicating with at each facility.

We also need accurate legal names and contact information. In some cases, the patient’s name may have changed since the specimens were originally stored. The clinics may have different names in their records. A clinic may belong to a larger network and have several locations with similar names.

Small differences can matter. We cannot assume that a clinic’s main office address is the same place where the laboratory stores specimens.

**Josh:** What is the first thing you do after receiving that information?

**Sasha:** We contact the shipping and receiving facilities.

We need to confirm that each facility recognizes the patient, has the appropriate records, and is willing to participate in the transfer. We also need the correct laboratory contacts because the front desk usually cannot answer technical questions about releasing or receiving cryogenic specimens.

Each clinic has its own requirements. One may need a release form. Another may need a storage agreement, physician approval, infectious disease records, payment, or other documentation.

The process becomes much easier once we know exactly what each side requires.

**Josh:** Is it usually clear who to contact?

**Sasha:** Not always.

Patients may give us the name of a doctor, nurse, financial coordinator, or front desk employee. That person may not be responsible for cryogenic specimen transfers.

We might call the general office, explain the situation, be transferred to the laboratory, leave a message, and then wait for someone to respond. Sometimes we learn that specimen transfers are handled by a separate records department or by someone at another location.

Finding the right person can be one of the first delays in the process.

**Josh:** What happens once you find the correct clinic contacts?

**Sasha:** We begin confirming the requirements and readiness of both facilities.

The sending clinic has to be willing and authorized to release the specimens. The receiving clinic has to agree to accept them. They may need to exchange records directly with each other.

We track what each party still needs to complete. That might include patient signatures, facility forms, account payments, medical records, storage agreements, or confirmation of the specimen inventory.

The administrative challenge is that all of those items may be coming through different channels.

**Josh:** What channels do the facilities use?

**Sasha:** Email, phone, fax, portals, and occasionally mailed forms.

A clinic may send one document by fax and confirm another by email. A patient may complete a form through a clinic portal that we cannot access. Someone may tell us over the phone that the case is approved, but we still need written confirmation.

That makes it difficult to see the complete state of a shipment without checking several places.

**Josh:** Do patients understand how much coordination is involved?

**Sasha:** Usually not at the beginning, and that is understandable.

From the patient’s perspective, the specimens are at one clinic and need to go to another clinic. It can sound like a normal pickup and delivery.

What they do not see is that both facilities have their own legal, clinical, financial, and administrative requirements. We cannot schedule the courier simply because the patient has requested the move.

That can be frustrating when the patient is working under a treatment deadline.

**Josh:** What kinds of deadlines are involved?

**Sasha:** A patient may have an upcoming treatment cycle, consultation, embryo transfer, or appointment with a new clinic.

The IVF process itself can include testing, ovarian stimulation, egg retrieval, fertilization, embryo development, genetic testing, and embryo transfer. Moving stored specimens may be only one part of a much larger medical schedule.

The patient may tell us that their doctor needs the embryos by a certain date. We then have to work backward and determine whether the clinics can complete all requirements in time.

**Josh:** What happens when a patient has an urgent deadline?

**Sasha:** We explain what is possible and identify what is still blocking the shipment.

We can prioritize communication and follow up more frequently, but we cannot skip clinic requirements. A rushed process that leaves out an authorization or receiving confirmation is not safe.

Sometimes patients hear that we use dedicated couriers and assume that means we can ship immediately. Dedicated transportation can make the physical journey faster, but it does not eliminate the preparation beforehand.

IVFCRYO uses medical couriers who carry the shipment directly from one facility to the other without unrelated stops. That reduces transportation risk, but both laboratories still need to be ready.

**Josh:** What is the most common reason a shipment is delayed?

**Sasha:** Waiting for a facility to complete or confirm something.

It may be a missing form, a balance that has not been paid, a record that has not been transferred, or a laboratory that has not confirmed its availability.

Sometimes everyone believes the case is ready, but one person is still waiting for a final approval.

That is why verbal assumptions are dangerous. We need a clear acknowledgment from each responsible party.

**Josh:** How do you track what is still missing?

**Sasha:** We maintain a case record and document our communication.

For each shipment, we need to know whether the patient has completed their responsibilities, whether the shipping clinic has approved release, whether the receiving clinic has approved acceptance, and whether the courier plan has been confirmed.

The difficulty is that not every step happens inside one system. Some information still has to be gathered from email threads, phone notes, documents, and clinic responses.

A lot of office management is making sure nothing disappears between those channels.

**Josh:** How much follow-up is required?

**Sasha:** It depends on the facilities.

Some clinics have an established transfer process and respond quickly. Others may need several calls or emails. Staff may be away, working in the laboratory, or handling urgent patient procedures.

We have to be persistent without becoming disruptive. We also need to keep the patient informed without blaming a clinic or creating conflict.

**Josh:** What do you tell a patient when a clinic is not responding?

**Sasha:** I would explain that we are still waiting for confirmation from the facility and that we have followed up.

The important thing is to be specific enough that the patient understands the status. Saying “the shipment is being processed” is not very helpful.

A better update would be that the shipping clinic has approved the transfer, but we are still waiting for the receiving laboratory to confirm its intake requirements.

That tells the patient what has been completed and what is still outstanding.

**Josh:** Do patients ever contact the clinics themselves?

**Sasha:** Yes. Sometimes that helps because the patient has an established relationship with the clinic.

But it can also create confusion when the patient receives a different answer than we do. A front desk employee may say everything is ready, while the embryology laboratory is still waiting for documentation.

We need to compare the information and make sure the approval came from the person who is actually responsible.

**Josh:** How do you manage the emotional side of these calls?

**Sasha:** We have to remember what is being transported.

The shipment may contain a patient’s only embryo, eggs collected after a difficult treatment cycle, or sperm stored before medical treatment. The specimens can be irreplaceable.

Patients are often already carrying a lot of anxiety from the IVF process. IVFCRYO’s own patient material describes the journey as emotionally and physically demanding.

When patients call, they may not only be asking where the courier is. They may be asking whether their chance to have a child is safe.

**Josh:** What does good patient communication look like?

**Sasha:** It is clear, calm, and honest.

We should tell the patient what is happening, what has been completed, what is still needed, and what happens next.

We should not overwhelm them with technical information that does not help them make a decision. But we also should not use vague reassurance.

IVFCRYO provides updates by phone, email, text message, and online shipment tracking. The challenge is making sure those updates are consistent across every channel.

**Josh:** What questions do patients ask most often?

**Sasha:** They ask whether their specimens will be safe, who will transport them, when they will arrive, and whether they can track the shipment.

They also want to know what happens if the shipping container fails or the courier is delayed.

Some patients ask about the CRYO Shield Protection Program. The shipping page describes protection options of $25,000 or $50,000 per client and tissue type.

We have to explain that protection carefully because it has specific terms and documentation requirements.

**Josh:** What makes explaining the service guarantee difficult?

**Sasha:** Patients may hear words such as “guarantee,” “insurance,” or “protection” and assume that every unexpected outcome is automatically covered.

The published CRYO Shield terms are much more specific. The order must contain accurate information. The shipping and receiving facilities must properly prepare and handle the shipping unit. The unit must be released to the courier on time. A qualifying claim also requires evidence that the unit arrived warmer than minus 130 degrees Celsius at the specimen storage level, measured with appropriate calibrated equipment.

There are also written evidence requirements and a 30-day claim submission period.

From an office perspective, that means accuracy and documentation are important from the beginning, not only when something goes wrong.

**Josh:** What information is especially important to get right on the order?

**Sasha:** Patient identity, tissue type, facility names, facility addresses, contact information, and the selected declared value.

We also need to know whether multiple tissue types are being transported. The protection options are described per client and per tissue type, so that distinction can matter.

An error that looks minor in the office could create a significant problem later.

**Josh:** What happens as the shipping date approaches?

**Sasha:** We reconfirm the schedule and the responsible contacts.

We need to know when the courier will arrive, who will release the container, who will receive it, and whether each facility will have qualified staff available.

The timing needs to work for both laboratories. It is not enough for the building to be open. Someone with the authority and training to handle the shipment needs to be present.

We also need a plan for what happens if the courier arrives early, late, or cannot immediately reach the designated contact.

**Josh:** How are the shipping containers prepared?

**Sasha:** The laboratories are responsible for properly preparing and loading the shipping unit.

Our office team coordinates the logistics, but we do not make clinical decisions or handle the specimens inside the laboratory.

This separation is important. We need to know that the unit is ready, but the clinic is responsible for identifying the specimens, placing them inside the unit, and documenting the release.

The CRYO Shield terms also make the responsibilities of the shipping and receiving facilities part of the claim requirements.

**Josh:** How do you know whether the shipment remains safe during transportation?

**Sasha:** IVFCRYO uses an IVFCRYO-Sense device to monitor the shipment.

The device can provide GPS location, internal shipper temperature, outside temperature, humidity, and atmospheric pressure while the shipment is moving.

From an office perspective, this gives us visibility that we would not have from ordinary courier tracking. We are not only seeing where the vehicle is. We are also seeing information about the conditions around the specimens.

**Josh:** Are you personally watching the temperature the entire time?

**Sasha:** The organization needs monitoring and escalation processes rather than relying on one person staring at a screen.

The system should make it clear when a reading is normal, when something requires closer attention, and when a critical response is needed.

IVFCRYO’s temperature-monitoring guidance emphasizes continuous monitoring, early warning alerts, critical alerts, remote access, escalation procedures, and backup support.

For the office team, the key question is who needs to be notified and what information they need to act.

**Josh:** Do patients see the temperature information?

**Sasha:** They can receive real-time shipment updates and track the shipment through the website.

There is a balance, though. Raw technical data can create anxiety when it is not explained.

A patient may see a temperature change slightly and assume there is an immediate emergency. A good interface should provide context and make the difference between normal conditions, a warning, and an actual safety issue clear.

**Josh:** What happens if the courier is delayed?

**Sasha:** We first determine why the delay occurred, where the courier is, and how the shipment conditions look.

Then we communicate with the receiving facility and determine whether staff will still be available. We may also need to update the patient.

A traffic delay may be manageable. A major weather event or facility closure may require a different response.

The important thing is having current information. Without GPS, environmental monitoring, and accurate facility contacts, we would be trying to make decisions with incomplete information.

**Josh:** What happens when the shipment arrives?

**Sasha:** The receiving laboratory accepts the shipping unit, inspects it, and moves the specimens into appropriate storage.

We need delivery confirmation, but simply seeing “delivered” in a courier system is not enough. We want to know that the correct laboratory personnel accepted the unit.

The receiving clinic may also need to document the condition of the container and take an immediate temperature measurement.

That documentation becomes especially important if there is visible damage or an unexpected reading.

**Josh:** What happens if there is a problem at delivery?

**Sasha:** We begin documenting immediately.

That may include the delivery time, the name of the person who received the unit, temperature readings, photographs, video, written statements, and monitoring data.

The CRYO Shield terms require specific evidence for a claim, including statements from the shipping and receiving facilities, proof of the temperature at the specimen storage level, and images of applicable damage.

Even when an issue does not become a formal claim, accurate documentation helps us understand what happened.

**Josh:** How do you avoid losing information during an emergency?

**Sasha:** We need a standard incident workflow.

The system should tell us what information to collect, who to contact, and how quickly each action needs to happen.

Without that structure, people may focus on solving the immediate problem and forget to record an important detail. Later, everyone is trying to reconstruct the event from memory.

An emergency is exactly when the process needs to be clearest.

**Josh:** Do equipment decisions affect the office team?

**Sasha:** Yes, even though we are not the people selecting or maintaining every piece of cryogenic equipment.

Reliable equipment reduces exceptions, urgent calls, and uncertainty. Monitoring systems also give us information we can use when coordinating with clinics.

IVFCRYO supplies cryogenic storage equipment and monitoring systems, including the IVFCRYO-One. The unit records nitrogen levels, nitrogen usage, evaporation rates, and temperatures at different levels. It also provides text and email alerts and includes redundant third-party monitoring.

The office team may help schedule installation, maintenance, service calls, or communication when a facility reports an equipment concern.

**Josh:** What happens when a clinic asks for advice about equipment?

**Sasha:** We need to understand what they are trying to accomplish and then connect them with the right technical person.

A small clinic may have different storage and capacity needs than a large fertility network. They may be concerned about space, automatic filling, monitoring, ergonomics, or backup systems.

The supplier guidance emphasizes evaluating construction quality, insulation, testing, temperature stability, nitrogen retention, monitoring, maintenance, replacement parts, and long-term support.

My role would be making sure the request is captured accurately and reaches someone who can provide a technically appropriate answer.

**Josh:** Where do mistakes happen most easily?

**Sasha:** They happen at handoffs.

A patient believes the clinic sent a form. The clinic believes we received it. We may have received the form but still need a signature. Everyone thinks the task is complete, but the case is actually still blocked.

The same thing can happen when a clinic says it is ready to receive the shipment. We need to know who said that, whether the laboratory agrees, and whether the correct person will be present on delivery day.

“Someone said it was handled” is not a reliable status.

**Josh:** What would make those handoffs more reliable?

**Sasha:** Every requirement should have a clear owner and a verifiable completion state.

Instead of a general status such as “waiting on clinic,” the case should say exactly what is missing, which clinic is responsible, who was contacted, when they were contacted, and what the next follow-up date is.

When a task is completed, the responsible party should acknowledge it. Sending a document and approving a document are not the same thing.

**Josh:** What does your ideal shipment management system look like?

**Sasha:** It would have one complete case view.

At the top, I would see the patient, tissue type, sending facility, receiving facility, target date, assigned coordinator, and current status.

Below that, I would see a checklist of requirements. Each item would have an owner, due date, status, and supporting document.

Once the shipment begins, the case would change into a live operations view. It would show the courier, pickup confirmation, location, internal temperature, expected arrival, receiving contact, delivery confirmation, and final laboratory acceptance.

Everything would be recorded in one audit trail.

**Josh:** Would the patient use the same interface?

**Sasha:** The patient would see a simpler version of the same case.

They would see what they need to complete, what IVFCRYO is working on, whether each clinic is ready, the expected shipping date, and live transportation updates.

They would not need to see every internal note, technical reading, or clinic document.

The goal would be transparency without making the patient responsible for managing the workflow.

**Josh:** What should be automated?

**Sasha:** Routine reminders and status updates.

The system could remind a clinic about an unanswered request. It could notify the coordinator when a document arrives. It could alert the patient when both facilities approve the transfer.

On shipping day, it could automatically send pickup, transit, delay, and delivery updates.

It could also flag inconsistent information, such as different patient names or facility addresses.

**Josh:** What should not be automated?

**Sasha:** Sensitive conversations and decisions that require judgment.

A system should not automatically tell a patient that their specimens may be compromised without a qualified person reviewing the information.

It should not decide whether the correct specimens were loaded or whether a clinic is clinically prepared to accept them.

Automation should make the facts easier to see. It should not pretend that complex medical and operational decisions are simple.

**Josh:** Which part of your job creates the most work?

**Sasha:** Repeated follow-up.

One missing approval may require several emails, calls, and status updates. Multiply that across many active shipments, and it becomes difficult to keep every case moving.

The work itself is not always complicated, but the volume of small dependencies is.

A system that showed exactly what was missing and automatically handled routine reminders would free the team to focus on exceptions and patient support.

**Josh:** Which part is most stressful?

**Sasha:** Unexpected changes on shipping day.

Before pickup, there is still time to resolve many issues. Once the courier is moving, every delay has an immediate effect.

You have a sensitive shipment, two clinics with operating hours, a courier on the road, and a patient waiting for updates.

That is when clear information and defined escalation paths matter most.

**Josh:** What would you change first?

**Sasha:** I would create a shared readiness checklist that both facilities must confirm before the courier is scheduled.

It would not be enough for our team to mark a clinic as ready. The responsible person at each facility would confirm that the required documents, staff, equipment, and timing are in place.

That would prevent a lot of last-minute surprises.

**Josh:** How would you measure whether the process had improved?

**Sasha:** I would measure how long it takes to move a case from order placement to shipment readiness.

I would also track the number of manual follow-ups, incomplete orders, clinic response times, rescheduled shipments, and shipping-day exceptions.

From the patient side, I would look at how often people contact us because they do not understand the status of their shipment.

A better process should reduce uncertainty for both the office team and the patient.

**Josh:** Is there anything people misunderstand about the office manager’s role in this process?

**Sasha:** People may think the administrative work is separate from specimen safety.

It is not.

The courier cannot make a safe delivery if the address is wrong. The receiving laboratory cannot accept the shipment if no qualified staff are available. A monitoring alert is not useful if no one knows who should receive it. A protection claim cannot be evaluated without accurate records.

The equipment and courier protect the physical shipment. The administrative process protects the chain around it.

**Josh:** What is the most important thing you want a patient to feel during the process?

**Sasha:** That someone understands the full case and is paying attention.

The patient should not feel like they are carrying information back and forth between two clinics. They should not have to wonder whether anyone saw their form or whether the courier arrived.

We cannot remove every risk or every difficult emotion from IVF. But we can make the shipping process organized, visible, and compassionate.

**Josh:** Thank you, Sasha.

**Sasha:** Thank you.
