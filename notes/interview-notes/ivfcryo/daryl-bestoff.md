# Interview with Daryl, COO at IVFCRYO, LLC

**Research focus:** Understanding the operational, organizational, and risk-management challenges involved in safely shipping reproductive specimens between cryobank facilities

**Josh:** Thanks for speaking with me, Daryl. Can you begin by explaining your role as COO?

**Daryl:** My responsibility is to make sure our services work consistently from an operational standpoint.

That includes the people, equipment, couriers, procedures, communication systems, monitoring technology, documentation, and vendor relationships needed to complete each shipment.

Don may establish the direction of the company, and the office team may coordinate individual cases, but my focus is making sure the entire operation can deliver what we promise repeatedly.

A successful shipment should not depend on one employee remembering every detail or personally rescuing the process at the last minute.

**Josh:** What does a successful shipment look like to you?

**Daryl:** The most obvious outcome is that the correct reproductive specimens arrive at the correct facility while remaining safely within cryogenic conditions.

But operationally, we need more than that.

The order needs to be accurate. Both facilities need to be ready. The shipping unit must be properly prepared. The courier must arrive at the correct location and time. The shipment must remain visible during transit. The receiving facility must accept it promptly. Every major handoff must be documented.

The patient should also understand what is happening without needing to repeatedly call us for answers.

Success means the shipment is safe, predictable, traceable, and handled with as little avoidable stress as possible.

**Josh:** What makes these shipments operationally difficult?

**Daryl:** We are coordinating a process across organizations that we do not directly control.

The patient, the originating clinic, the receiving clinic, the courier, and IVFCRYO each have different responsibilities. The clinics may use different paperwork, technologies, schedules, and internal approval processes.

We can design our own workflow, but we still have to interact with dozens of outside workflows.

The challenge is creating a dependable chain from organizations that may never have worked together before.

**Josh:** Where does IVFCRYO’s responsibility begin and end?

**Daryl:** That distinction needs to be very clear.

IVFCRYO provides cryogenic shipping units and coordinates access to dedicated medical couriers, monitoring, tracking, and customer support. The clinics remain responsible for identifying, preparing, loading, releasing, receiving, and storing the reproductive specimens.

The published CRYO Shield Service Guarantee makes an important legal distinction. It defines the formal service as the provision of the shipping unit and describes logistical planning as a complimentary benefit rather than the guaranteed service itself.

From an operational perspective, however, we still need the full experience to work. A customer will not separate equipment, scheduling, transportation, and communication when deciding whether the shipment was successful.

**Josh:** Does that create tension between the legal definition of the service and the customer experience?

**Daryl:** It can.

Contracts need to state exactly what each organization is responsible for. That protects everyone from assuming that IVFCRYO controls clinical decisions or specimen handling inside a facility.

At the same time, we cannot operate as though our only responsibility is handing someone a container. Patients come to us because they need help getting sensitive material safely from one place to another.

The operational challenge is providing meaningful coordination while preserving clear boundaries around what clinics, couriers, and customers are required to do.

**Josh:** How do you standardize a process when every clinic operates differently?

**Daryl:** We standardize the parts we control and create structured checkpoints for the parts we do not.

For example, every shipment should have a verified originating facility, receiving facility, tissue type, patient identity, responsible contacts, target schedule, courier plan, readiness status, and monitoring plan.

The exact clinic forms may differ, but the operational questions remain the same.

Has the sending facility approved release?

Has the receiving facility approved acceptance?

Will qualified staff be present?

Has the shipping unit been prepared?

Is the courier schedule confirmed?

Are the required contacts available?

The system needs to convert different clinic procedures into a consistent operational readiness model.

**Josh:** What happens when those checkpoints are not standardized?

**Daryl:** Employees create their own processes.

One coordinator may maintain detailed notes. Another may rely on email flags. Someone else may keep a separate spreadsheet or depend on memory.

That can work when the volume is low and the most experienced people are available. It becomes risky as the company grows.

The goal is not to remove judgment. It is to make sure routine safety steps do not vary based on who happens to be managing the shipment.

**Josh:** How do you determine that a shipment is ready to schedule?

**Daryl:** Readiness should be treated as a formal state, not a general impression.

The patient must have completed their responsibilities. Both clinics must confirm their responsibilities. The pickup and delivery contacts must be identified. The courier route and timing must be feasible. The equipment must be available and suitable for the expected duration.

Only after those elements are confirmed should the case move from preparation into scheduled transportation.

One of the worst operational patterns is scheduling first and hoping the remaining requirements are completed before pickup.

**Josh:** Why does that happen?

**Daryl:** Usually because someone is trying to meet a patient’s deadline.

There may be an upcoming embryo transfer, treatment cycle, relocation, or clinic appointment. The patient is understandably anxious to move forward.

The office team may reserve a courier slot to protect the date while still waiting for a clinic approval.

Sometimes that works. Sometimes it creates a last-minute cancellation, extra cost, and more anxiety.

We need to distinguish between a requested date, a provisional plan, and a confirmed shipment. Those cannot all look the same in the system.

**Josh:** How do dedicated medical couriers fit into the operating model?

**Daryl:** They reduce unnecessary handling and transportation variability.

IVFCRYO’s shipping service uses dedicated medical couriers rather than standard parcel networks. The courier carries the shipment directly between facilities without unrelated delivery stops.

That gives us more control over routing, pickup, delivery, communication, and chain of custody.

A general parcel network may involve sorting centers, several vehicles, warehouse transfers, and handlers who do not understand the contents. Those additional handoffs are unacceptable risks for this type of shipment.

**Josh:** Does using a dedicated courier eliminate transportation risk?

**Daryl:** No. It reduces certain risks.

A dedicated courier can still encounter traffic, weather, vehicle problems, incorrect facility information, unavailable staff, or changes in clinic hours.

The difference is that we have more direct visibility and communication. We know who is carrying the unit and can adjust the plan when something changes.

No individual control eliminates risk. Safety comes from layers.

**Josh:** What are those layers?

**Daryl:** Reliable equipment is one layer.

Proper preparation by the sending facility is another. Dedicated transportation reduces handoffs. GPS and temperature monitoring provide visibility. Clear facility contacts make escalation possible. Receiving procedures confirm the condition of the shipment at arrival.

Documentation provides accountability across the entire process.

If one layer fails, the others should help us detect the issue, contain it, and respond appropriately.

**Josh:** How is the shipment monitored?

**Daryl:** IVFCRYO uses its IVFCRYO-Sense device to track GPS location, internal shipper temperature, outside temperature, humidity, and atmospheric pressure during transportation.

That allows us to see both where the shipment is and what is happening around it.

Traditional courier tracking may tell you that a package left one location and is expected at another. That is not enough for reproductive tissue. We need environmental visibility throughout the trip.

**Josh:** What operational problems does monitoring solve?

**Daryl:** It shortens the time between a condition changing and someone becoming aware of it.

A cryogenic unit does not always move directly from normal operation to complete failure. Problems may begin with a slight increase in evaporation, reduced nitrogen levels, insulation deterioration, or inconsistent sensor readings.

Continuous monitoring lets us identify trends and respond before conditions become critical.

It also provides a record. After delivery, we can review what happened rather than relying only on a reading taken at the beginning and another taken at the end.

**Josh:** What makes a monitoring system operationally effective?

**Daryl:** The sensor itself is only one part.

You need reliable data transmission, defined warning thresholds, critical thresholds, escalation paths, responsible employees, backup contacts, and procedures for verifying that an alert reflects a real condition.

IVFCRYO’s monitoring guidance recommends continuous automated monitoring, early warnings, critical alerts, remote access, escalation procedures, and backup support.

An alert without a response plan is simply information arriving during a crisis.

**Josh:** How do you prevent alert fatigue?

**Daryl:** Alerts need to reflect urgency.

An informational update should not look or sound like a critical temperature warning. A small change that remains within normal conditions should not wake several people in the middle of the night.

We need separate levels for normal status, early warning, required action, and emergency response.

We also review repeated alerts. If one device frequently generates false or unhelpful notifications, employees will eventually stop trusting it.

**Josh:** Who is responsible when an alert occurs?

**Daryl:** That should be established before the shipment begins.

The first responder may be someone in operations who validates the reading and contacts the courier. A technical employee may review the equipment data. The shipping or receiving clinic may need to prepare for an expedited handoff.

A serious event may require executive involvement, legal review, documentation, customer communication, and contact with an alternate facility.

The escalation path cannot begin with everyone asking who owns the problem.

**Josh:** What information do you need during a transportation exception?

**Daryl:** We need the shipment location, internal temperature, direction of the temperature trend, expected travel time, shipping unit specifications, facility operating hours, courier status, and available alternate destinations.

We also need to know who has authority to make decisions.

The response to a short traffic delay is different from the response to a vehicle breakdown several hours from the destination.

The system should bring the relevant information together without forcing the operations team to search through emails and separate dashboards.

**Josh:** How important is equipment selection?

**Daryl:** It is foundational.

Cryogenic equipment operates near minus 196 degrees Celsius. The supplier needs to demonstrate manufacturing quality, insulation performance, vacuum integrity, temperature stability, nitrogen retention, regulatory awareness, and long-term reliability.

Price matters, but the purchase cost is a small part of the total risk.

We also evaluate serviceability, replacement parts, technical support, monitoring compatibility, maintenance needs, upgrade options, and future capacity.

The equipment needs to be supported throughout its useful life.

**Josh:** Why is supplier support part of operations?

**Daryl:** Because equipment eventually needs inspection, maintenance, troubleshooting, and replacement.

A tank may begin using nitrogen faster than expected. A controller may need calibration. A sensor may fail. A facility may need to expand capacity or upgrade its monitoring system.

IVFCRYO’s equipment guidance recommends evaluating long-term support and the supplier’s ability to provide monitoring upgrades and future expansion options.

A supplier that disappears after the sale transfers the entire operational burden to the clinic.

**Josh:** How does IVFCRYO’s own equipment support that approach?

**Daryl:** IVFCRYO offers equipment from several cryogenic brands, including IVFCRYO-One, IC-Bio, and MVE. The IVFCRYO-One includes an integrated alert and monitoring system intended to be operational when the unit is installed.

Integrated monitoring reduces the need to assemble several disconnected tools after purchase.

From an operations perspective, we want equipment data, alerts, maintenance history, and service responsibilities to be clearly defined from the beginning.

**Josh:** Is redundancy important?

**Daryl:** Yes.

You do not want the same failure to disable both the primary system and the backup.

That applies to temperature sensors, communication methods, employees, courier contacts, and facility plans.

A primary monitoring system may provide detailed operational data, while an independent backup confirms that the environment remains safe.

Redundancy does increase complexity, so it needs to be tested. A backup that nobody reviews or knows how to use is not real protection.

**Josh:** How do you approach maintenance?

**Daryl:** Maintenance should be scheduled based on the equipment, usage, manufacturer guidance, monitoring trends, and the risk associated with failure.

IVFCRYO recommends professional inspection of cryogenic units, including manual-fill tanks, to evaluate vacuum performance and signs of deterioration.

We also look for changes in nitrogen use, evaporation rates, temperature consistency, alarms, physical damage, and service history.

The goal is to replace or repair equipment before it becomes an emergency.

**Josh:** How do you decide when equipment should be replaced?

**Daryl:** Age is one factor, but it is not enough by itself.

We consider reliability history, nitrogen efficiency, vacuum performance, temperature stability, repair frequency, parts availability, and whether the monitoring technology still meets our needs.

IVFCRYO’s maintenance guidance suggests considering replacement when repairs become repetitive, when costs approach a meaningful portion of replacement value, or when monitoring shows accelerating nitrogen loss or unstable temperatures.

Waiting for complete failure is not an acceptable replacement strategy.

**Josh:** How does the CRYO Shield Service Guarantee affect operations?

**Daryl:** It reinforces the importance of accurate orders, documented facility responsibilities, immediate inspection, calibrated temperature measurement, and complete records.

A qualifying claim requires an error-free order, proper preparation by the facilities, timely handoff, no documented preexisting damage, and an arrival temperature warmer than minus 130 degrees Celsius at the specimen storage level. The measurement must be taken with an appropriately calibrated and traceable thermometer.

That means the claims process begins long before a claim exists.

If our order data and handoff records are incomplete, it may be impossible to reconstruct the event later.

**Josh:** Does the service guarantee change how shipments are managed?

**Daryl:** It should not change the standard of care. Every shipment should be managed carefully whether or not a customer selects a particular protection amount.

But the guarantee creates additional documentation responsibilities.

The declared value needs to be recorded correctly. The shipping and receiving facilities need to understand their obligations. Any damage or temperature concern must be documented immediately.

The published terms require written evidence, temperature records, facility statements, photographs or video when applicable, and proof supporting the specimen’s declared value. Claims must be submitted within 30 days.

**Josh:** Is it difficult to place a monetary value on reproductive material?

**Daryl:** Yes. The emotional and personal value cannot truly be replaced with money.

The declared value and service guarantee provide a financial mechanism for a defined loss. They do not replace an embryo, egg, or sperm specimen.

Operationally, we need to explain that distinction clearly. We should not use financial protection as a substitute for discussing the actual safeguards and responsibilities involved.

**Josh:** What are the greatest business risks for IVFCRYO?

**Daryl:** The greatest risk is harm to reproductive material and the people who trusted us to help move it.

Beyond that, we have legal risk, reputational risk, financial risk, vendor risk, cybersecurity risk, and operational continuity risk.

A single serious failure could involve several of those at once.

That is why we cannot evaluate risk only by asking whether shipments usually arrive successfully. We need to examine how failures could happen, how quickly they would be detected, and whether the organization is prepared to respond.

**Josh:** How do you manage vendor risk?

**Daryl:** We evaluate couriers, equipment suppliers, monitoring providers, communications systems, and other partners based on their ability to support the actual operating requirements.

For couriers, that includes availability, training, communication, reliability, geographic coverage, escalation procedures, and chain-of-custody practices.

For equipment suppliers, it includes quality documentation, performance history, maintenance support, parts availability, monitoring compatibility, and upgrade paths.

We also avoid becoming dependent on knowledge that exists only inside one external organization.

**Josh:** What does chain of custody mean operationally?

**Daryl:** It means we can identify who had responsibility for the shipping unit at each meaningful stage.

We should know who prepared it, who released it, which courier accepted it, when the courier departed, who received it, and when the specimens were moved into storage.

IVFCRYO’s shipping guidance emphasizes documented chain of custody, GPS tracking, status updates, and confirmation that the receiving clinic accepted the shipment.

Chain of custody should not end with a delivery scan at the front desk.

**Josh:** Where are the weakest handoffs?

**Daryl:** The transition between administrative approval and laboratory readiness is one.

An office employee may say a clinic is ready, but the embryology team may not have reviewed the case.

Pickup is another vulnerable point. The courier may arrive, but the correct laboratory employee may not be available.

Delivery has the same problem. A building can accept a package without the laboratory taking control of it.

We need named contacts and explicit acknowledgments at each point.

**Josh:** What role does the patient play in the operation?

**Daryl:** The patient authorizes the transfer, provides information, completes required forms, and makes decisions related to the order.

But the patient should not have to coordinate the laboratories.

They may be navigating testing, medication, egg retrieval, fertilization, embryo development, genetic testing, and embryo transfer. The transportation process is occurring within a much larger and emotionally demanding IVF journey.

Our operation should reduce their administrative burden rather than adding another complicated system for them to manage.

**Josh:** How do you balance transparency with the risk of causing anxiety?

**Daryl:** We provide useful context.

Patients should know when the shipment is scheduled, when it has been picked up, where it is, whether it remains within expected conditions, and when the receiving laboratory accepts it.

But we should not present raw technical data without explaining what it means.

A small environmental change may be normal. A warning may be under review. A true critical condition requires a different type of communication.

Transparency means helping the patient understand the state of the shipment, not simply exposing every internal data point.

**Josh:** What metrics would you use to evaluate operations?

**Daryl:** I would separate safety, reliability, efficiency, and customer experience.

For safety, I would track temperature excursions, monitoring gaps, equipment failures, chain-of-custody exceptions, and incomplete receiving documentation.

For reliability, I would examine on-time pickups, on-time deliveries, rescheduled shipments, courier exceptions, and shipments that began without every readiness requirement confirmed.

For efficiency, I would track the time from order to readiness, manual follow-ups, clinic response times, employee touches per case, and time spent resolving exceptions.

For customer experience, I would review patient status inquiries, complaints, satisfaction, and whether communications were delivered at the appropriate stages.

**Josh:** Would you set a goal of eliminating every delay?

**Daryl:** No. Some delays are appropriate.

A shipment should be delayed when a clinic is not ready, information is inconsistent, equipment is questionable, weather creates unacceptable risk, or the receiving facility cannot safely accept the specimens.

The goal is to eliminate preventable delays and identify necessary delays early.

A fast shipment is not successful if speed required ignoring a safety concern.

**Josh:** How do you investigate an operational failure?

**Daryl:** We first protect the specimens and support the patient.

Then we preserve the evidence. That includes monitoring data, courier records, communications, photographs, facility statements, order information, equipment history, and timestamps.

After the immediate issue is contained, we reconstruct the sequence of events.

We look beyond the final mistake. We ask why the process allowed the mistake to reach the customer.

Was a required field missing?

Was the procedure unclear?

Did the system fail to alert someone?

Was the employee overloaded?

Did a clinic provide conflicting information?

Did we rely on one person’s knowledge?

The purpose is not only to identify who made an error. It is to prevent the same conditions from producing another one.

**Josh:** How do you prevent employees from hiding mistakes?

**Daryl:** Leadership has to distinguish between honest reporting and reckless behavior.

If employees believe they will be punished for raising concerns, they will wait until problems are impossible to hide.

We need a culture where people stop a shipment when something looks wrong, document near misses, and ask for help early.

Accountability still matters. But the organization learns more from a reported near miss than from a quiet workaround that happened to succeed.

**Josh:** What does training involve?

**Daryl:** Employees need role-specific training.

A coordinator needs to understand readiness requirements, documentation, patient communication, and escalation.

Operations staff need to understand monitoring, equipment limitations, courier procedures, and incident response.

Anyone communicating with patients needs to understand the emotional context and avoid making unsupported promises.

Training should include realistic exceptions, not only the ideal process.

**Josh:** How do you know whether training worked?

**Daryl:** Completion records are not enough.

We look at whether employees follow the process, recognize problems, document cases correctly, and respond appropriately during simulations or real events.

We can also review recurring mistakes. If several employees misunderstand the same requirement, that is probably a training or process-design problem, not several unrelated individual failures.

**Josh:** How should technology support the operation?

**Daryl:** We need a shared case-management system rather than several disconnected records.

Every shipment should have a readiness view, document status, assigned responsibilities, communications history, courier plan, monitoring data, delivery confirmation, and audit trail.

The system should automatically surface missing information and conflicting data.

On shipping day, it should become an operational command center. Employees should see where the courier is, what the internal temperature is, who is receiving the shipment, and what actions are required.

**Josh:** What should be automated?

**Daryl:** Routine checks, reminders, status messages, data validation, and escalation routing.

The system can identify that a clinic has not responded, a required document is missing, a courier is approaching pickup, or a temperature has crossed a warning threshold.

It can also make sure that the right people receive updates.

Automation should reduce memory-dependent work.

**Josh:** What should remain under human control?

**Daryl:** Clinical decisions, specimen identification, release authorization, acceptance into storage, interpretation of serious incidents, and sensitive patient communication.

A system can show that a form is present. It may not know whether the form is clinically sufficient.

It can detect a temperature trend. A qualified person must decide what that trend means in the context of the unit and route.

Technology should improve judgment, not create the illusion that judgment is no longer necessary.

**Josh:** What would you change first about the current process?

**Daryl:** I would establish a formal, shared readiness gate before scheduling.

Every required party would confirm that their responsibilities are complete. The system would prevent a case from being represented as confirmed when it is still waiting for approval.

That one change would reduce rescheduling, wasted courier time, last-minute calls, and patient confusion.

It would also make performance measurable. We could see exactly where cases become blocked and how long each dependency takes.

**Josh:** What is the second priority?

**Daryl:** A unified exception-management workflow.

When a shipment is delayed or a monitoring alert occurs, the system should automatically assemble the relevant information, identify the response level, notify the correct people, and begin an incident record.

Today, experienced employees often know what to do because they have handled similar situations before.

We need to turn that experience into a repeatable organizational capability.

**Josh:** What does scaling IVFCRYO responsibly mean?

**Daryl:** It means increasing capacity without reducing control.

We cannot simply add more shipments and ask the same people to work faster.

We need better tools, clearer roles, more reliable data, tested vendors, equipment capacity, backup coverage, documented procedures, and performance monitoring.

Growth should make the operation more mature, not more dependent on heroic employees.

**Josh:** What is the most important operational principle for this work?

**Daryl:** Nothing should be assumed.

Do not assume a document was approved because it was sent.

Do not assume a clinic is ready because the front desk said it was.

Do not assume a container is functioning because it feels cold.

Do not assume a shipment is complete because a courier marked it delivered.

Critical steps need to be verified and documented.

**Josh:** What do people outside the company misunderstand about operations?

**Daryl:** They often see the courier as the operation.

The vehicle moving between two facilities is only the visible part.

The real operation includes equipment preparation, facility readiness, authorizations, scheduling, monitoring, communication, chain of custody, delivery acceptance, documentation, maintenance, vendor management, incident response, and patient support.

The safest shipment is usually the result of a lot of work that the patient never needs to see.

**Josh:** What should the patient feel at the end of a shipment?

**Daryl:** They should feel that the process was controlled.

They should know when the shipment moved, where it was, when it arrived, and that the receiving clinic accepted it.

They should not feel that success depended on them calling everyone involved and forcing the process forward.

We cannot remove the emotional weight of transporting reproductive material. But we can prevent operational uncertainty from making that weight heavier.

**Josh:** Thank you, Daryl.

**Daryl:** Thank you.
