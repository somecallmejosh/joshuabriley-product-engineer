# Interview with Don, Owner of IVFCRYO, LLC

**Research focus:** Understanding the challenges of safely managing reproductive specimen shipments between cryobank facilities

**Josh:** Thanks for speaking with me, Don. To begin, can you tell me what IVFCRYO does and how shipping fits into the business?

**Don:** IVFCRYO provides cryogenic services for fertility clinics, cryobanks, and patients. That includes shipping reproductive tissue, cryogenic storage equipment, monitoring systems, maintenance, storage, and consulting.

Shipping is a major part of what we do. We transport embryos, eggs, sperm, and other reproductive tissue between IVF clinics and storage facilities. Our team has decades of experience in the fertility industry, and we have managed more than one million reproductive tissue shipments.

The important thing to understand is that these are not ordinary packages. Sometimes we are transporting a patient’s last embryo or their only remaining reproductive material. There may be no way to replace what is inside the container. That changes the level of responsibility involved.

**Josh:** Can you walk me through a typical shipment?

**Don:** Usually, a patient needs to move specimens from one clinic or cryobank to another. They might be changing fertility clinics, moving to another state, working with a surrogate, using donor material, or preparing for a future embryo transfer.

Once an order is placed, we assign someone from our customer experience team to the case. That person collects the necessary information and communicates directly with the shipping and receiving facilities.

The two facilities have to agree that the transfer is authorized and that they are ready. The sending clinic must prepare the specimens and place them into the shipping unit correctly. The receiving clinic must be ready to accept the shipment, inspect the unit, verify the specimens, and move them into appropriate storage.

We then coordinate the courier, monitor the shipment, provide updates, and confirm delivery. Our goal is to keep the patient from having to manage two clinics, a courier, documentation, and tracking on their own.

**Josh:** What makes that process difficult?

**Don:** The challenge is that a shipment might look like one transaction to the patient, but it is actually a chain of separate responsibilities.

You have the patient, the shipping clinic, the receiving clinic, our coordination team, and the courier. Each party may use different systems, different terminology, and different internal procedures.

One facility may require a particular consent form. Another may require infectious disease documentation, physician authorization, storage agreements, identity records, or proof that outstanding fees have been paid. Some facilities respond quickly. Others may need repeated follow-up.

The shipment cannot safely move until every required party is ready. When information is missing, outdated, or incorrect, the entire process can stop.

**Josh:** Who is ultimately responsible for making sure everything is ready?

**Don:** Responsibility is shared, and that is one of the biggest coordination challenges.

The patient has to provide accurate information and complete the necessary authorizations. The originating facility has to properly prepare the shipping unit and release it to the correct courier on time. The receiving facility has to be available and prepared to accept it.

We coordinate the logistics and provide the shipping unit, courier, tracking, and support. But we cannot replace the clinical responsibilities of the facilities. The clinic staff are responsible for identifying, handling, loading, receiving, and storing the actual specimens.

A safe process has to make those boundaries clear. It cannot leave people assuming that someone else completed a critical step.

**Josh:** How do you verify that the correct specimens are being shipped?

**Don:** The clinics have established identification and chain-of-custody procedures. The originating clinic identifies the specimens, matches them to the patient documentation, and loads them into the shipping unit.

The courier does not open the container and handle the reproductive material. The courier transports the secured unit between authorized facilities.

At delivery, the receiving facility confirms the shipment, inspects the unit, verifies the documentation, and transfers the specimens into its storage system.

From a logistics perspective, we need accurate patient information, correct facility information, the right pickup and delivery contacts, and confirmation that both facilities have approved the move. A mistake in any of those details can create a delay or an unacceptable risk.

**Josh:** What type of courier do you use?

**Don:** We use dedicated medical couriers.

“Dedicated” means that the courier is carrying that shipment directly between the two facilities without making unrelated delivery stops. “Medical” means the courier is trained to handle reproductive tissue shipments and can properly enter medical facilities for pickup and delivery.

That is very different from placing a cryogenic unit into a general parcel network. A normal carrier may move a package through sorting centers, loading areas, multiple vehicles, and different handlers. That introduces additional handoffs and opportunities for delays or improper handling.

Our approach is designed to reduce those handoffs and provide the fastest practical route from one clinic to the other.

**Josh:** What are the greatest physical risks during transportation?

**Don:** Temperature is the most obvious concern, but it is not the only one.

The shipping unit has to maintain cryogenic conditions for the entire journey. It also has to be prepared correctly, handled correctly, kept secure, and delivered within the expected time window.

Cryogenic storage operates at extremely low temperatures. The IVFCRYO materials describe long-term storage near minus 196 degrees Celsius and identify approximately minus 130 degrees Celsius as an important biological safety threshold. The objective is to keep the reproductive material well below that threshold throughout transport.

A unit could experience physical damage, unexpected warming, excessive delays, or environmental changes. Failures do not always begin with a dramatic event. They can start with changes in nitrogen retention, insulation performance, internal temperature, or sensor behavior.

That is why we do not want to rely only on someone looking at the container at pickup and delivery.

**Josh:** How do you monitor the shipment?

**Don:** We use the IVFCRYO-Sense device to provide visibility into the conditions surrounding the shipment.

It can report the GPS location, the temperature inside the shipper, the outside ambient temperature, humidity, and atmospheric pressure. That information can be monitored while the shipment is moving.

Patients can also receive real-time shipment updates through the IVFCRYO website. We communicate by phone, email, and text during the process.

The point is not simply to display a dot on a map. We want to know where the shipment is and whether the environment inside the unit remains appropriate.

**Josh:** Why is continuous monitoring so important?

**Don:** Because a snapshot only tells you what happened at one moment.

Someone can take a temperature reading before pickup and another after delivery, but that leaves a large gap. Continuous monitoring gives you a record of what happened during the trip.

It helps identify changes early and provides evidence that conditions remained stable. Automated monitoring also reduces the chance of a missed manual check and makes it possible to notify someone when a value begins moving toward an unsafe range.

A good monitoring system should have early warnings, critical alerts, remote access, escalation procedures, and backup support. The alert should happen before the temperature reaches a dangerous point, not after the problem has already occurred.

**Josh:** Does monitoring eliminate the risk?

**Don:** No. Monitoring improves visibility. It does not make equipment infallible.

You still need a properly prepared shipping unit, trained clinic staff, a qualified courier, accurate documentation, and a realistic route and schedule.

That same principle applies to permanent storage equipment. A facility needs reliable construction, strong insulation, stable nitrogen retention, alarms, temperature monitoring, maintenance, and technical support. The equipment and the operational process have to work together.

Technology can tell you that something is wrong. People still need to know who receives the alert, what the alert means, and what action to take.

**Josh:** What makes cryogenic equipment suitable for this work?

**Don:** Reliability has to be demonstrated rather than assumed.

Facilities should look at manufacturing quality, testing documentation, temperature stability, nitrogen retention, vacuum integrity, regulatory expectations, certifications, maintenance requirements, and long-term performance.

They should also consider the support behind the equipment. Can they reach someone when nitrogen usage starts increasing? Are replacement parts available? Is there a clear maintenance program? Can the monitoring system be upgraded? Will the supplier help install and verify the equipment?

Cryogenic equipment is not a one-time purchase that can be forgotten after delivery. It needs to be treated as a supported system throughout its useful life.

**Josh:** How does IVFCRYO approach equipment and monitoring inside a facility?

**Don:** One example is the IVFCRYO-One storage unit. It is designed to provide both liquid submersion storage and temperatures below minus 150 degrees Celsius for vapor storage applications.

It automatically monitors and records information such as liquid nitrogen levels, usage, evaporation rate, and temperatures at different levels inside the tank. It includes Wi-Fi-based alerts through email and text, along with a redundant third-party monitoring system.

The broader idea is to reduce repetitive manual work while providing more data and more than one layer of protection.

**Josh:** Are there operational benefits beyond safety?

**Don:** Yes. Safety comes first, but workflow matters because poor workflow can create safety problems.

If embryologists are constantly moving tanks, bending over equipment, manually checking levels, clearing fog, recording readings, and filling containers, that consumes time and creates opportunities for mistakes.

The IVFCRYO-One, for example, was designed with increased capacity, automatic filling, integrated monitoring, improved visibility inside the unit, and a more ergonomic working height.

Those details may sound secondary, but the easier it is for trained staff to follow the correct process, the more consistently that process will be followed.

**Josh:** What happens if a temperature issue is detected during transport?

**Don:** The response depends on the reading, the location, the remaining travel time, and whether the data indicates a true threat to the specimens.

Not every variation is an emergency. That is why you need defined warning levels and escalation procedures. An early warning might trigger closer monitoring or a call to the courier. A critical alert could require immediate intervention and coordination with the nearest appropriate facility.

The worst time to decide who is responsible is after an alarm has already occurred. The escalation path should be established before pickup.

**Josh:** What happens when the shipment arrives?

**Don:** The receiving clinic should inspect the shipping unit immediately, verify the condition of the container, take any required temperature readings, confirm the specimen documentation, and transfer the material into appropriate cryogenic storage.

The delivery confirmation alone is not enough. We need to know that the container arrived at the right facility and that qualified staff accepted responsibility for it.

If there is visible damage, an unexpected temperature reading, or another discrepancy, it needs to be documented immediately with measurements, written statements, photographs, or video.

**Josh:** IVFCRYO offers a service guarantee. How does that work?

**Don:** Customers can select a declared value and a corresponding level of protection through the CRYO Shield program. The shipping page currently describes options of $25,000 or $50,000 per client and tissue type.

The service guarantee has specific conditions. The order must contain accurate information. The shipping and receiving facilities must meet their responsibilities. The unit must be properly prepared and released on time. The shipment must be accepted without documented preexisting damage.

The published guarantee identifies an arrival temperature warmer than minus 130 degrees Celsius at specimen storage level as one of the conditions required for a claim. That temperature must be measured using appropriate calibrated equipment.

A claim also requires documentation, including evidence of the order, facility statements, temperature records, photographs or video of applicable damage, and evidence supporting the declared value.

**Josh:** Why is so much documentation required?

**Don:** Because the shipment passes through several organizations, and any investigation has to reconstruct what happened.

We need to know whether the order was correct, whether the unit was properly prepared, whether it was damaged before pickup, how it was handled, what the monitoring data showed, what condition it was in at delivery, and how the receiving clinic measured the temperature.

Without documentation, you may have several parties remembering the event differently. A reliable record protects the patient, the facilities, the courier, and us.

Documentation should not be viewed as paperwork added after the real work. It is part of the safety system.

**Josh:** What documentation problems occur most often?

**Don:** Incorrect facility information is a common source of trouble. The name of a clinic may be similar to another location within the same network. A patient may provide a general office phone number instead of the embryology laboratory contact.

Authorizations may be incomplete. Names may not match exactly across records. A clinic may still be waiting for a storage agreement, medical clearance, infectious disease testing, or payment.

Sometimes the problem is not that information is missing. It is that the information exists in an email, a fax, a clinic portal, and someone’s notes, but no one has a complete view of the case.

**Josh:** How much visibility does the patient have?

**Don:** We want the patient to understand where the shipment stands without making them responsible for managing every operational detail.

They should know whether both clinics have approved the transfer, whether the shipment has been scheduled, when pickup occurs, where the shipment is during transit, and when it has been delivered.

But they should not have to repeatedly call two clinics and try to translate laboratory procedures between them. Our team works directly with the facilities and provides updates through phone, email, text, and online tracking.

**Josh:** What questions do patients ask most often?

**Don:** They want to know whether their specimens will be safe, who will be carrying them, how long the trip will take, whether they can follow the shipment, and what happens if something goes wrong.

They also ask why the process takes time before the actual shipping day. From their perspective, the clinics already know where the embryos are and where they need to go. It seems like it should be possible to simply pick them up.

What they do not always see is the authorization, record verification, release approval, receiving approval, container preparation, scheduling, and chain-of-custody work that has to happen first.

**Josh:** How does the emotional side of IVF affect the shipping experience?

**Don:** It affects everything.

IVF is already physically and emotionally demanding. Patients may have gone through testing, medication, monitoring, egg retrieval, fertilization, embryo development, genetic testing, and unsuccessful transfers.

By the time they are moving an embryo, that embryo may represent years of medical treatment and a significant financial and emotional investment. It may be their only remaining embryo.

The shipping process may be one operational step to us, but to the patient it is tied to fear, hope, grief, and the possibility of building a family. The IVFCRYO patient materials describe the larger IVF journey as deeply personal and often filled with anxiety and uncertainty.

We need to communicate clearly without being cold, and we need to be reassuring without making promises that no responsible company could make.

**Josh:** Is there a tension between transparency and patient anxiety?

**Don:** Absolutely.

Patients want visibility, and they should have it. But raw technical information without context can increase anxiety.

For example, a sensor reading may move slightly while remaining well within a safe range. If a patient sees the number change but does not understand the threshold, they may think the specimen is in immediate danger.

A useful tracking system has to explain status in understandable language. It should distinguish between normal variation, something being reviewed, and a condition requiring action.

Transparency is not simply showing every data point. It is giving people the information they need to understand what is happening.

**Josh:** Can you describe a shipment that does not go according to plan?

**Don:** Imagine a courier arrives at the shipping clinic, but the laboratory cannot release the specimens because one authorization is missing.

The courier has a schedule. The receiving clinic has arranged for staff to be available. The shipping unit has been prepared for a specific transport window. A missing document can affect every part of that plan.

Another example would be severe weather or an unexpected road closure. Even with a dedicated courier, the route may change. In that case, we need to understand the unit’s remaining capacity, the current internal temperature, the courier’s location, and the receiving clinic’s hours.

The important issue is not whether unexpected situations occur. They will. The issue is whether everyone can see the same information and respond through a clear escalation process.

**Josh:** Where do handoffs tend to break down?

**Don:** Handoffs break down when the next person does not know that they are now responsible.

A clinic employee may send a form and assume the case is approved. The coordinator may be waiting for confirmation from an embryologist. The embryologist may believe the receiving facility still needs to provide documentation.

The same problem can occur at delivery. A front desk employee may know a package is coming, but that does not mean qualified laboratory staff are prepared to receive cryogenic specimens.

Every major handoff needs an explicit acknowledgment. “Sent” is not the same thing as “received and approved.”

**Josh:** What tools are involved in managing all of this?

**Don:** There are several categories of tools.

We have customer and order information, facility contacts, scheduling, communication records, courier coordination, GPS tracking, environmental monitoring, delivery confirmation, and documentation.

The clinics may have their own electronic medical records, laboratory systems, inventory systems, email procedures, and release forms.

The challenge is creating one operational picture without pretending that all of those external systems are integrated. Our team often has to bring information together across several channels.

**Josh:** Where is there still too much manual work?

**Don:** Follow-up is one area. Someone may need to call or email a facility several times to confirm approval or identify a missing item.

Another area is checking whether every prerequisite has been completed before scheduling. When that depends on several messages and notes, the coordinator has to mentally reconstruct the case.

Exception management is also labor intensive. When something changes, the coordinator has to determine who needs to know, what downstream appointments are affected, and whether a new approval is required.

Automation could help with reminders, status changes, document validation, and notifications. But sensitive clinical decisions and specimen handling still require qualified people.

**Josh:** Imagine you could redesign the shipment management system. What would you want to see?

**Don:** I would want one case record that clearly shows the patient, the tissue type, the originating facility, the receiving facility, and the current stage of the transfer.

It should show what has been completed and what is still blocking the shipment. Each requirement should have an owner, a status, a timestamp, and supporting documentation.

On shipping day, the same record should show pickup confirmation, courier identity, GPS location, internal temperature, outside conditions, expected arrival, delivery confirmation, and final acceptance by the receiving laboratory.

I would also want a complete audit trail. Every approval, correction, alert, message, and handoff should be recorded without forcing someone to search through separate email threads.

**Josh:** Would everyone see the same information?

**Don:** They would see information from the same underlying case, but not necessarily the same interface or level of detail.

The coordinator needs the full operational view. Clinic staff need documentation, scheduling, chain-of-custody, and specimen-handling information relevant to their role. The courier needs pickup, routing, contact, and delivery instructions.

The patient needs a clear status, outstanding actions assigned to them, expected timing, progress updates, and confirmation that the transfer was completed.

Access has to be based on role. Medical and identifying information should only be visible to people who need it.

**Josh:** What notifications should happen automatically?

**Don:** The system should notify the appropriate person when an authorization is missing, a clinic has not responded, a shipment has been approved, a pickup is approaching, the courier has arrived, the shipment has departed, an environmental reading crosses a warning level, the estimated arrival changes, or delivery is completed.

But notifications need to be meaningful. If the system sends too many routine alerts, people will stop paying attention.

We need clear differences between an informational update, an action request, an early warning, and a critical alert.

**Josh:** Which decisions should never be completely automated?

**Don:** Anything involving specimen identification, release approval, clinical suitability, specimen loading, or acceptance into storage needs qualified human oversight.

A system can verify that a required field is present. It cannot independently determine that the clinic selected the correct embryo.

A system can surface a temperature trend. An experienced person has to evaluate the circumstances and determine the appropriate response.

The purpose of automation should be to help people see the situation and act sooner, not remove accountability.

**Josh:** What is the biggest challenge from your perspective?

**Don:** Maintaining control across organizational boundaries.

Inside one company, you can define a process, train everyone, and use a shared system. In these shipments, the patient, two clinics, a courier, and IVFCRYO may all operate independently.

We have to create a reliable chain across those separate organizations. That means accurate information, clear ownership, verified handoffs, continuous monitoring, strong communication, and documented accountability.

**Josh:** Which challenge creates the most administrative work?

**Don:** Confirming readiness between the two facilities.

The driving portion of a shipment may be completed in a day. The coordination beforehand can take much longer because every authorization and facility requirement has to be resolved.

A better system would make those dependencies visible and reduce the amount of time spent asking, “What are we still waiting for?”

**Josh:** Which challenge affects patients the most?

**Don:** Uncertainty.

They can accept that a careful process takes time. What is difficult is not knowing who has the case, whether a clinic responded, whether the shipment is still on schedule, or whether the specimens arrived safely.

Good communication does not eliminate the emotional weight of the situation, but it prevents avoidable uncertainty from adding to it.

**Josh:** If you could solve one problem first, what would it be?

**Don:** I would create a truly shared readiness workflow.

Before a shipment can be scheduled, the system would clearly confirm that the patient, sending clinic, receiving clinic, courier plan, shipping unit, and required documentation are all ready.

Each party would know what they need to do. The coordinator could immediately see where the process is blocked. The patient would receive a clear update without having to chase anyone.

That would reduce delays, prevent scheduling errors, and make shipping day much more predictable.

**Josh:** How would you measure whether a new system improved the process?

**Don:** I would look at the time from order placement to shipment readiness, the number of manual follow-ups, the number of shipments rescheduled because of incomplete requirements, and the time it takes to resolve exceptions.

I would also measure whether patients contact us because they do not understand the status of their shipment.

On the safety side, I would review monitoring coverage, missing data, temperature warnings, chain-of-custody completeness, and whether the receiving facility confirms acceptance promptly.

The outcome we want is not simply faster shipping. It is a more predictable process with fewer preventable errors and less uncertainty.

**Josh:** Is there anything important that people commonly misunderstand about reproductive specimen shipping?

**Don:** People sometimes think the container is the service.

The container is essential, but it is one part of a larger safety system. You need the right equipment, proper preparation, trained handlers, direct transportation, real-time monitoring, facility coordination, accurate documentation, and a clear response plan.

The other misunderstanding is that this is only a technical problem. It is also a human problem. The reproductive material inside the unit may represent someone’s only remaining chance to have a biological child.

Every operational decision has to respect that reality.

**Josh:** Thank you, Don.

**Don:** Thank you. The more clearly we understand the full process, the better we can protect the specimens and support the people who have trusted us with them.
