# Interview Conversation with Austin

**Interviewer:** Thanks for speaking with me today, Austin. I’m trying to understand how recruiters at staffing agencies find and manage job openings when those openings are spread across different systems.

**Austin:** Sure. That is a big part of the job, especially when you recruit nationally.

**Interviewer:** Can you tell me a little about your role?

**Austin:** I’m a headhunter for a staffing agency. I recruit workers for employers across the United States.

I work with candidates, hiring managers, account managers, and sometimes third-party staffing programs. My job is to find qualified people, get them interested, make sure they meet the requirements, and move them through the submission process.

**Interviewer:** What types of roles do you recruit for?

**Austin:** It varies. We handle healthcare roles, operations positions, technical jobs, and some specialized contract work.

A lot depends on the client. Some clients need a large number of workers. Others may only have one highly specialized position.

**Interviewer:** How do you find out which jobs are available?

**Austin:** That is where it gets complicated.

Some jobs come into our applicant tracking system. Some are posted in vendor management systems. Some come through email from an account manager. Others are listed in client portals, spreadsheets, or shared documents.

There is no single source that contains everything.

**Interviewer:** How many systems do you check?

**Austin:** On a normal day, probably six or seven directly. Across the agency, there are many more.

Some recruiters specialize in certain clients or industries, so they may have access to systems I do not use regularly.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through how you start your day.

**Austin:** I usually start by checking our ATS and email.

I look for new openings, updates to jobs I am already working on, candidate responses, and messages from account managers.

Then I check the major VMS platforms and client portals.

**Interviewer:** Do you have to log into each system separately?

**Austin:** Yes. Most of them have separate logins. Some require multifactor authentication.

It can take a while just to get everything open.

**Interviewer:** Do you keep all the systems open throughout the day?

**Austin:** Usually, yes. I may have a dozen browser tabs open.

That includes our ATS, LinkedIn, email, job boards, candidate databases, and the client systems.

**Interviewer:** How do you know which jobs are worth focusing on?

**Austin:** I look at urgency, pay, location, candidate availability, client relationship, and how likely the job is to fill.

Some jobs look good at first, but then you realize the requirements are unrealistic or the pay is too low for the market.

**Interviewer:** Who decides which jobs are the highest priority?

**Austin:** Sometimes the account manager tells us. Sometimes there is a priority flag in the system.

Other times, recruiters make that decision based on experience.

**Interviewer:** Can you walk me through the last new opening you worked on?

**Austin:** Yesterday I received a nursing job in Texas.

It came through a VMS, but I first heard about it in an email from the account manager.

The email said it was urgent and that the client wanted submissions that day.

**Interviewer:** What did you do next?

**Austin:** I opened the VMS and searched for the job.

The title in the email did not exactly match the title in the system, so it took a few minutes to find it.

Then I reviewed the description and compared it with what was in our ATS.

**Interviewer:** Were the records consistent?

**Austin:** Not completely.

The shift was different in the ATS. The VMS said nights, but our system said rotating shifts.

The start date was also different.

**Interviewer:** Which source did you trust?

**Austin:** I usually treat the client system as the source of truth, but even that can be wrong.

I messaged the account manager and asked for clarification.

**Interviewer:** How long did it take to get an answer?

**Austin:** About an hour.

That does not sound like much, but for a high-priority job, an hour can matter.

**Interviewer:** Did you wait before contacting candidates?

**Austin:** I started searching, but I did not present the job as a firm opportunity.

I told one candidate that I had a possible night-shift role and that I was confirming the details.

**Interviewer:** Does that happen often?

**Austin:** Yes. Recruiters are often working before every detail is confirmed because waiting can mean losing the candidate or the job.

**Interviewer:** What information do you need before you can confidently contact someone?

**Austin:** I need the job title, location, schedule, pay range, start date, contract length, and major requirements.

For healthcare jobs, I also need the specialty, required licenses, certifications, years of experience, and sometimes facility-specific requirements.

**Interviewer:** Which details are most often missing?

**Austin:** Pay is sometimes unclear. The shift can be vague. The exact location is not always obvious.

Requirements are also a problem. A job description may say two years of experience, but then we learn the manager wants three years and prior experience with a specific system.

**Interviewer:** What happens when those requirements appear later?

**Austin:** We waste time.

The recruiter wastes time. The candidate wastes time. The account manager may review a submission that never had a chance.

It also makes the candidate feel like we did not understand the job.

**Interviewer:** Does that affect your credibility with candidates?

**Austin:** Absolutely.

Candidates expect us to know what we are recruiting for. If we keep changing the details, they lose trust.

**Interviewer:** How consistent are job titles across systems?

**Austin:** They are not consistent at all.

One job might be called a customer support specialist in one system and a client services representative in another.

Healthcare roles are even more complicated because abbreviations vary.

**Interviewer:** How does that affect your searches?

**Austin:** It means I cannot rely on one title.

I use several search terms. I also search by skills, certifications, or similar past roles.

If the job is categorized incorrectly in our ATS, I may not see it at all.

**Interviewer:** Do you ever find the same job listed more than once?

**Austin:** Constantly.

It might be in the ATS, in a VMS, and in an email. Sometimes it has different identification numbers.

The pay may also be slightly different, which makes it hard to know whether it is a duplicate or a separate opening.

**Interviewer:** What happens when recruiters do not realize it is the same job?

**Austin:** Multiple recruiters may contact the same candidate about it.

That is not a good look.

It can also create internal arguments about who owns the candidate or the submission.

**Interviewer:** How does your agency decide candidate ownership?

**Austin:** We have rules, but it can still get messy.

Usually, the recruiter who first contacted the candidate or has the existing relationship gets ownership.

But if the candidate responds to someone else first, there may be a dispute.

**Interviewer:** Do duplicate jobs make that problem worse?

**Austin:** Yes. Especially when recruiters think they are working on different openings.

**Interviewer:** How do you know whether a job is still open?

**Austin:** That is one of the biggest problems.

Some systems update the status correctly. Others do not.

Sometimes I only learn a job closed when I try to submit a candidate.

**Interviewer:** What happens then?

**Austin:** I have to go back to the candidate and explain that the position is no longer available.

That is frustrating, especially if they already updated their resume, completed paperwork, or rearranged their schedule to speak with me.

**Interviewer:** Does that happen often?

**Austin:** More often than it should.

**Interviewer:** How quickly do some jobs fill?

**Austin:** Very quickly.

For high-demand contract roles, the client may receive enough candidates within an hour or two.

Some jobs technically remain open in the system, but the client has already stopped reviewing submissions.

**Interviewer:** So the displayed status may not reflect the real status?

**Austin:** Correct.

A job can say open, but it is effectively closed.

**Interviewer:** How do you learn that?

**Austin:** Usually from the account manager. Sometimes other recruiters share what they know.

A lot of information travels through informal channels.

**Interviewer:** What kinds of informal channels?

**Austin:** Teams messages, Slack, group chats, email threads, or quick calls.

Someone may say, “Do not work on that one. They already have twenty submissions.”

That information may never be recorded in the ATS.

**Interviewer:** Does that make it harder for someone who was not part of the conversation?

**Austin:** Yes. They may keep working on the job because the official system still says it is active.

**Interviewer:** How much of your day is spent finding and verifying jobs?

**Austin:** Probably more than a quarter of the day on a bad day.

It is not just finding the job. It is checking the details, looking for updates, comparing systems, and asking questions.

**Interviewer:** What would you rather spend that time doing?

**Austin:** Talking to candidates.

Good recruiting is about relationships. You need to understand what someone wants, what they are qualified for, and what would make them consider a move.

That is hard to do when you are buried in administrative work.

**Interviewer:** How do you currently find candidates?

**Austin:** We use our ATS, LinkedIn, job boards, referrals, old applications, and outreach tools.

Sometimes I remember a candidate from a previous search and contact them directly.

**Interviewer:** Does your system automatically suggest candidates for new jobs?

**Austin:** It does, but the matches are not always useful.

It may match based on a title or keyword without understanding the context.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me an example?

**Austin:** It might match someone who worked as a director of nursing to a bedside nursing contract because both records contain the word “nursing.”

Technically there is a match, but it is not a realistic one.

**Interviewer:** What makes a candidate match useful to you?

**Austin:** The system needs to consider skills, location, pay expectations, work authorization, availability, licenses, certifications, and whether the person is actually interested in that type of work.

Past behavior also matters. Someone may be qualified for contract work but only want permanent positions now.

**Interviewer:** Is that information current in your ATS?

**Austin:** Not always.

Candidate profiles go stale quickly. People move, gain experience, change salary expectations, or stop looking.

**Interviewer:** How do you verify the information?

**Austin:** Usually by contacting the person.

That is another reason automated matching only gets you so far.

**Interviewer:** How many candidates might you contact for one opening?

**Austin:** It depends on the role.

For a common role, I may contact fifty or more people to get a few serious responses.

For a highly specialized role, there may only be ten realistic candidates in the market.

**Interviewer:** Do you contact candidates manually?

**Austin:** A mix.

We use automated email or text campaigns for some searches, but personalized outreach usually performs better.

**Interviewer:** Do automated messages ever create problems?

**Austin:** Yes. If the job data is wrong, the same wrong information may be sent to hundreds of people.

Automation makes mistakes scale faster.

**Interviewer:** What is the most damaging incorrect detail?

**Austin:** Pay is probably number one.

If you advertise one rate and then tell the candidate it is lower, they feel misled.

Location is also a major issue. A city name can represent a very large area.

**Interviewer:** What other information changes frequently?

**Austin:** Start dates, interview requirements, number of openings, and whether the client will consider remote or hybrid work.

For contracts, extensions and schedule requirements may change too.

**Interviewer:** How are those changes communicated to you?

**Austin:** Through a combination of system updates and messages from the account manager.

There is not always a clean notification.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever missed an important update?

**Austin:** Yes.

I once submitted a candidate after the required certification changed. The update had been added to the notes in the VMS, but it did not flow into our ATS.

The candidate was rejected immediately.

**Interviewer:** How did that affect the candidate?

**Austin:** They were disappointed and asked why I had submitted them when they did not qualify.

It made me look careless even though the information I had was incomplete.

**Interviewer:** What did you do after that?

**Austin:** I started checking the original VMS record before every submission for that client.

**Interviewer:** Does every recruiter do that?

**Austin:** Probably not.

That is one of those personal workarounds you develop after getting burned.

**Interviewer:** What other workarounds do you use?

**Austin:** I keep a spreadsheet with priority jobs and notes. I also save important client messages in a folder.

For certain clients, I keep a list of requirements that are not always included in the job description.

**Interviewer:** Is that information shared with the rest of the team?

**Austin:** Some of it is. Some is just for me.

**Interviewer:** What happens if you are out of the office?

**Austin:** Another recruiter can see the jobs in the ATS, but they may not understand all the context.

They might not know which jobs are truly urgent or which details are outdated.

**Interviewer:** Does the agency have a standard process for handing off your work?

**Austin:** We have a process, but it depends on how much time you have before being out.

An unexpected absence is harder.

**Interviewer:** How does recruiting across the entire country add complexity?

**Austin:** Time zones are a big part of it.

A job may be posted late in the afternoon on the East Coast, but candidates on the West Coast are still working.

Licensing rules, pay expectations, cost of living, and labor laws also vary by state.

**Interviewer:** Do systems account for those differences?

**Austin:** Not very well.

A national search looks simple on paper, but a role that pays well in one state may be uncompetitive in another.

**Interviewer:** How do you decide whether a pay rate is realistic?

**Austin:** Experience, market data, job board research, and candidate feedback.

Candidates usually tell you very quickly when the rate is not competitive.

**Interviewer:** What happens when the client refuses to adjust the rate?

**Austin:** The job stays open.

Recruiters may stop prioritizing it because they know candidates will reject it.

**Interviewer:** Is that lack of recruiter activity visible to the client?

**Austin:** Sometimes.

They may see that there are no submissions, but they may not understand why.

**Interviewer:** Does your team report that feedback?

**Austin:** We try to. The account manager usually communicates it.

But feedback can get simplified as it moves between the recruiter, account manager, staffing program, and hiring manager.

**Interviewer:** What would you want the hiring manager to understand?

**Austin:** I would want them to see the real market response.

For example, we contacted forty qualified people, twenty said the pay was too low, ten were unavailable, and five wanted remote work.

That is much more useful than simply saying we had no candidates.

**Interviewer:** Can your current systems show that?

**Austin:** Not easily.

Some activity is tracked, but candidate responses often live in email, LinkedIn, or text messages.

**Interviewer:** What kind of reporting does your agency track?

**Austin:** Submissions, interviews, placements, time to fill, recruiter activity, response rates, and revenue.

We also track how quickly recruiters respond to new openings.

**Interviewer:** How accurate are those reports?

**Austin:** They are useful, but they do not tell the whole story.

A recruiter can show a lot of activity without doing meaningful work. Another recruiter may spend two hours having a strong conversation with a rare candidate, and that is harder to measure.

**Interviewer:** What part of the current process would you automate first?

**Austin:** I would create one reliable job feed.

Every active job would appear in one place with the latest details, a clear source, and a timestamp showing when it was last updated.

**Interviewer:** What would come next?

**Austin:** Better candidate matching.

Not just keyword matching. I would want a ranked list of people who are qualified, available, and likely to be interested.

**Interviewer:** Would you trust a system to contact those candidates automatically?

**Austin:** For some roles, maybe.

But I would want control over the message and the audience.

I would not want the system sending generic messages to executive candidates or people I have strong relationships with.

**Interviewer:** What would you want to review before outreach happened?

**Austin:** The job details, candidate list, message, and any reason the system thinks the person is a match.

**Interviewer:** Why is the explanation important?

**Austin:** Because I need to catch bad assumptions.

If the system matched someone based on an outdated certification or a job they had ten years ago, I need to know that.

**Interviewer:** What would make you distrust a new system?

**Austin:** Missing jobs, stale jobs, bad matches, and incorrect pay information.

Once recruiters stop trusting a tool, they go back to their spreadsheets and personal methods.

**Interviewer:** What would make you trust it?

**Austin:** Consistency.

I would need to see that it is more accurate than the process we have now.

I would also want to know where the data came from and when it was last verified.

**Interviewer:** Imagine the ideal system. What would you see when you started your day?

**Austin:** I would see a list of new jobs that match my recruiting area.

I would see which existing jobs changed overnight, which ones closed, and which ones need clarification.

Each job would have a ranked candidate list and an explanation of why those candidates match.

**Interviewer:** What would happen when you selected a candidate?

**Austin:** I would see their current contact information, work history, previous conversations, availability, pay expectations, and any licensing or location issues.

I could contact them without switching between five tools.

**Interviewer:** What would happen after the candidate responded?

**Austin:** The response would be recorded automatically.

If they were interested, the system would guide me through the next steps. If they declined, it would record why.

That feedback could improve future matching.

**Interviewer:** What is the biggest challenge in your work today?

**Austin:** Speed without sacrificing accuracy.

We are expected to move quickly, but the information is scattered and sometimes unreliable.

**Interviewer:** What part of your job takes more effort than clients probably realize?

**Austin:** Finding the right person is only part of it.

We also have to verify the job, verify the candidate, keep both sides interested, manage expectations, collect documents, and make sure nothing changed along the way.

**Interviewer:** What do people designing recruiting tools usually misunderstand?

**Austin:** They assume recruiting is a database search.

It is really a relationship business supported by data.

A tool can help me find people and organize information, but it cannot treat every candidate like a record in a spreadsheet.

**Interviewer:** Of all the problems we discussed, which would you solve first?

**Austin:** I would make the job information trustworthy.

Candidate matching does not matter if the job is already closed or the details are wrong.

**Interviewer:** How would you know the solution was working?

**Austin:** I would spend less time checking systems and more time talking to people.

We would have fewer rejected submissions caused by incorrect requirements.

Candidates would also stop hearing different details from different recruiters.

**Interviewer:** Who else should I speak with to understand this process?

**Austin:** An account manager would be important because they sit between the recruiter and the client.

I would also speak with a candidate. They experience the confusion from the other side.

A recruiting operations manager would be helpful too because they understand the systems and reporting.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything else you think I should know?

**Austin:** Recruiters create workarounds because they are trying to move quickly, not because they want to ignore the official process.

If the official tools do not give them current information, they will build their own system.

**Interviewer:** That is helpful. Thank you for your time, Austin.

**Austin:** You’re welcome.
