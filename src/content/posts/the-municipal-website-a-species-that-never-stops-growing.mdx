<p class="lede">Most of the web lives in tidy, well-kept gardens. The logo sits in the top left, the navigation is easy to find, and forms tell you what went wrong. But at the edge of the map, past the last design system, lives a creature that has never been groomed. It's the municipal website, and it's been growing, undisturbed, for about twenty-five years.</p>

Let's approach slowly. It's renewing a dog license.

## A creature that grows by addition

The municipal website was never designed all at once. It grew one department at a time. Parks and Recreation added a page for summer camps. Public Works added a page for road closures. The Clerk's Office added a page called "Documents and Resources," and then, a few years later, a second page called "Forms and Documents."

Nothing is ever removed. A notice about the 2019 leaf collection schedule is still here, three clicks deep, and it's still marked "NEW!"

## Distinctive markings

Visitors can identify the municipal website by several features. The homepage has a rotating photo of the town hall, a weather widget, and a grid of thirty-two icons of roughly equal importance. One of them says "I Want To…"

The search bar sits at the top of every page. It returns a 2014 meeting agenda for almost any query. Experienced visitors skip it entirely and search on Google instead, which finds the right page on the city's own website faster than the city's website can.

## The PDF burrow

Many of the municipal website's most important features live underground, in PDFs. A permit application, a business license renewal, and a request for a backyard chicken coop all begin the same way: download, print, fill out by hand, scan, and email to an address that may or may not still be monitored.

Some of these PDFs are fillable. Most are scans of a photocopy of a form that was typed on a typewriter. A screen reader, arriving here, finds nothing at all.

## The symbiotic payment portal

When it's time to pay a water bill or a parking ticket, the municipal website does something remarkable. It sends the visitor to an entirely different website, run by a payment vendor, with a different logo, a different color scheme, and a different login.

The two species depend on each other. The city doesn't have to build a payment system, and the vendor collects a convenience fee on every transaction. The visitor, who only wanted to pay a parking ticket, now has a new password to forget.

## Why it survives

It's tempting to think the municipal website is a rare oddity, but it's one of the most common species on the web. Counties, school districts, utilities, courts, and hospital billing departments all have their own versions. For many people, these are the websites that matter most, because they're the only way to get something done.

The habitat explains a lot. Most cities have a small web team, sometimes a team of one. Each department owns its own pages, and nobody owns the whole website. Content management systems are chosen through procurement and kept for a decade. Every change has to fit a budget that was approved last year.

The municipal website isn't neglected because nobody cares. It's the result of many people caring about their own small piece of it.

## A healthier habitat

There are signs of change. GOV.UK showed that a government website can be organized around tasks people need to finish, instead of the departments that handle them. In the United States, the U.S. Web Design System gives agencies a shared set of accessible components, so they don't have to build their own. And in 2024, the Department of Justice adopted a rule requiring state and local government websites to meet WCAG 2.1 AA, the same accessibility standard many private companies already follow.

The best improvements are often small. A page named "Pay a parking ticket" instead of "Revenue Services." A web form instead of a PDF. A search bar that finds the current page instead of the oldest one. None of these require a full redesign, and each one saves a resident a phone call.

The sun sets over the town hall. Somewhere on the website, a new page is added, and the old one stays exactly where it is.
