<p class="lede">A resident needs to pay a water bill. It's a simple errand, the kind that should take two minutes. They type the name of their town into a search engine, click the first result, and step into one of the wildest habitats on the web.</p>

"Welcome to the Official Website of the Township," says the homepage, over a slideshow of the town hall in four different seasons.

## Distinguishing features

The municipal website is easy to identify in the field. Look for a scrolling alert ticker announcing a road closure from last spring. Look for a left-hand menu with sixty links, sorted by department, then by whoever asked for a link most recently. Look for a "Quick Links" section that contains more links than the rest of the page.

Most specimens also carry a weather widget, a photo of the mayor, and a button labeled "How Do I…?" that leads to a second, longer menu.

## Not as rare as it looks

To a visitor who spends most of their time on polished commercial websites, the municipal website looks like an anomaly, a creature that somehow survived an extinction event the rest of the web went through years ago.

It isn't rare at all. The United States alone has roughly 90,000 local governments, including counties, cities, townships, school districts, and water authorities, and nearly all of them have a website. Add the state agencies, the courts, and the motor vehicle offices, and this is one of the most common species on the web. Most people simply don't encounter it until they need to renew a permit.

## How it grows

The municipal website isn't designed so much as it accumulates. Each department adds its own pages, in its own style, whenever it has something to announce. Nobody removes anything, because nobody is sure who owns it, and some of it might be required by law.

Redesigns happen about once a decade, when a budget line opens up. A vendor installs a new content management system, the old pages are copied across, and the cycle starts again. The people who maintain these sites are usually doing it alongside three other jobs, and they deserve more sympathy than they get.

## The PDF thicket

Deep in the site, the visitor will find the PDF thicket. Permit applications, meeting agendas, budgets, and the water bill payment form all live here as documents that have to be downloaded, printed, filled in by hand, and returned in person or by mail.

Some of these PDFs are scans of paper forms, which means a screen reader finds nothing inside them at all. Some are 40 pages long, and the one field the visitor needs is on page 31.

## The residents who depend on it

What makes this species different from a bad shopping site is that nobody can take their business elsewhere. There's only one place to register to vote in your county, one place to appeal a property assessment, and one place to find out whether school is canceled.

The people who rely on these sites most are often the ones they serve least well: older residents, people on older phones with slow connections, people who use screen readers, and people reading in a second language. A confusing interface here doesn't cost a sale. It costs someone their benefits appointment.

## Signs of recovery

There's good news in the region. GOV.UK showed years ago that a government website can be calm, plain, and easy to use, and its design patterns have been studied by teams around the world. In the United States, the U.S. Web Design System gives agencies tested components and guidance, so they don't have to start from scratch.

The legal climate is changing too. In 2024, the U.S. Department of Justice issued a rule requiring state and local government websites to meet the WCAG 2.1 AA accessibility standard. For a lot of municipal websites, that means the PDF thicket is finally being cleared.

The healthiest specimens share a few traits. They organize pages around what residents need to do, like "Pay a bill" and "Report a pothole," instead of around the org chart. They put forms on the web instead of in PDFs. And they have someone whose job it is to say no to a new link on the homepage.

Back in the township, the resident has found the payment form under Departments, then Public Works, then Utilities, then Billing, then Forms. It's a PDF. They print it, find a stamp, and head to the mailbox.
