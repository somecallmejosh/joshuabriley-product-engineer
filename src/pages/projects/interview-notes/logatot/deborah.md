**Interviewer:** Thank you for speaking with me today, Deborah. I am researching the experience of managing a home-based childcare program. I am especially interested in the administrative work that happens behind the scenes, including attendance, meals, reporting, reimbursements, licensing, and funding.

There are no right or wrong answers. I am interested in what you actually experience, including what works well and what causes problems.

To start, can you tell me a little about your childcare program?

**Deborah:** Sure. I run a licensed childcare program out of my home. I have been doing this for about 14 years. I usually care for six children during the day, although the number changes depending on school schedules and whether I have part-time children.

Most of the children are between about 18 months and five years old. I also have two school-age children who come before school, after school, or during school vacations.

I have one assistant who helps me three days a week. The rest of the time, it is mostly me.

**Interviewer:** What does a typical day look like?

**Deborah:** The first child usually arrives around 6:45 in the morning. By 8:00, most of the children are here.

We do breakfast, free play, a morning activity, outside time if the weather is good, lunch, naps, snacks, and then pickups start in the afternoon. The school-age children come back later in the day.

The childcare part is only one part of the job. I am also preparing meals, cleaning, communicating with parents, tracking attendance, recording what the children ate, and making sure I have the paperwork I need.

Then, after everyone leaves, I usually have more paperwork to finish.

**Interviewer:** How much of your time is spent on administrative work?

**Deborah:** More than people probably realize.

During the day, I might spend a few minutes here and there recording meals, attendance, naps, or messages. But those minutes add up. Then I probably spend another hour or two each week reviewing records, preparing claims, organizing receipts, and checking deadlines.

Some weeks are much worse than others. If a report is due or I have a licensing visit coming up, it can take several evenings.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through how you track children arriving and leaving.

**Deborah:** I use an application for attendance, but I also keep a paper backup.

Parents are supposed to sign their children in and out. Some of them are good about it, but some forget. Sometimes they are in a hurry. Sometimes I am helping another child, and I do not notice until later.

When that happens, I have to remember the exact time the child arrived or left. Usually I can get close, but I do not like relying on memory.

**Interviewer:** What information do you need to record?

**Deborah:** At a minimum, I need the arrival time and departure time. For younger children, I also track meals, snacks, naps, diaper changes, and sometimes bathroom use.

If a child has medication, an injury, a fever, or some kind of behavior issue, that has to be documented too.

Parents want daily updates, but some of the same information is also required for licensing or reimbursement. The problem is that it is not always recorded in one place.

**Interviewer:** Do you ever enter the same information more than once?

**Deborah:** All the time.

I may record attendance in the childcare application, write meal information on a paper worksheet, and then enter that information again for the food program.

If a parent asks for an update, I might also send it through a text message or another parent communication app.

It feels like I am entering the same child, the same date, and the same activity several times.

**Interviewer:** Why do you still use paper if you have an application?

**Deborah:** Paper is faster in the moment.

If I am feeding six children, I can put a checkmark next to their names much faster than I can unlock my phone, open an app, find the right screen, and enter everything.

The application is useful when I have time to sit down. It is not always useful when I am actively supervising children.

I also do not completely trust technology. Apps stop working. Passwords expire. The internet goes down. I need a backup.

**Interviewer:** What happens when you cannot record something immediately?

**Deborah:** I try to write a quick note somewhere. Sometimes that means a sticky note or a mark on the meal sheet.

The danger is that I tell myself I will remember, and then three other things happen. A child spills something. A parent calls. Someone needs a diaper change. By the time I sit down, I am trying to reconstruct the day.

That is where mistakes happen.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me an example?

**Deborah:** A few months ago, I forgot to record a snack for two children. I served the snack, but I did not mark it down.

When I prepared my food program claim, it looked like those children had not been served. I could not claim reimbursement for those meals because I did not have the record.

It was not a huge amount of money by itself, but those mistakes add up.

**Interviewer:** How do you communicate daily updates to parents?

**Deborah:** It depends on the family.

Some parents like messages during the day. Some only want a quick conversation at pickup. I usually send pictures or short updates if something important happens.

For babies and toddlers, parents usually want to know about meals, naps, diapers, and mood. Older children do not need as much detail.

The challenge is that pickup time is busy. Several parents may arrive around the same time, and I am trying to supervise the children while also explaining how everyone’s day went.

**Interviewer:** Have communication problems ever caused confusion?

**Deborah:** Yes. Schedule changes are probably the biggest problem.

A parent might text me that a child will arrive late, but then I forget to update the calendar. Or one parent tells my assistant something, but the message does not get back to me.

There have also been times when a parent thought they told me about an absence, but I never saw the message.

It is not usually anyone’s fault. The information is just spread across too many places.

**Interviewer:** What attendance records are you required to maintain?

**Deborah:** I have to keep daily attendance records showing when each child arrived and left.

For children receiving assistance, attendance is especially important because payment may depend on approved schedules, actual attendance, or program rules.

I also need records for licensing, taxes, food reimbursement, and sometimes parent billing.

**Interviewer:** Are the rules different for private-pay families and subsidized families?

**Deborah:** Yes.

With private-pay families, the payment agreement is mostly between the family and me. They pay based on the contract, even if the child misses a day, unless we have agreed to something different.

With subsidized care, I have to follow program rules. The parent may have an approved schedule, and I have to report attendance correctly.

If something changes with the family’s eligibility, I may not find out right away.

**Interviewer:** Has that ever affected your payment?

**Deborah:** Yes.

I once had a child continue attending for more than a week after the family’s authorization had changed. The parent did not understand what had happened, and I was not notified immediately.

I provided the care, but part of it was not reimbursed. Then I had to decide whether to ask the parent for the difference, even though they could not afford it.

That puts providers in a terrible position.

**Interviewer:** How predictable are subsidy payments?

**Deborah:** They are predictable when everything goes correctly.

The problem is that a small paperwork issue can delay payment. A missing attendance record, a change in eligibility, or a form submitted incorrectly can hold things up.

My expenses do not stop while I wait. I still need food, supplies, utilities, and money to pay my assistant.

**Interviewer:** What reports are you required to complete?

**Deborah:** I have attendance reports, meal claims, income records, licensing documents, training records, enrollment forms, emergency contacts, and health records.

There are also forms connected to subsidy payments and tax records.

Some reports are monthly. Others are yearly. Some only come up during an inspection, renewal, or audit.

**Interviewer:** Which reports take the most time?

**Deborah:** The food program claim probably takes the most consistent effort because it requires daily tracking.

Licensing paperwork can also take a lot of time, especially when several forms are expiring around the same time.

The most stressful work is usually anything connected to money because a mistake can mean losing reimbursement.

**Interviewer:** Can you walk me through how meal reporting works?

**Deborah:** I participate in the Child and Adult Care Food Program.

I have to document which children were present, what meals and snacks were served, and whether the food met the program requirements.

I also plan menus. I need to make sure the meal includes the required components.

At the end of the reporting period, I submit a claim based on the records.

**Interviewer:** What makes that process difficult?

**Deborah:** The meal itself is not the difficult part. I already feed the children.

The hard part is documenting everything correctly while I am serving food, supervising, cleaning, and helping children.

If I forget to mark one child or enter the wrong meal, I may lose reimbursement.

There are also rules about serving sizes and meal components. Those rules can change based on the child’s age.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever had a claim rejected or adjusted?

**Deborah:** Yes. Usually it is because something was incomplete or did not match another record.

For example, if attendance shows that a child was absent, but the meal form shows that the child ate lunch, that creates a problem.

Sometimes it is a simple data entry mistake. But it still takes time to investigate and correct.

**Interviewer:** Is it easy to understand why a claim was changed?

**Deborah:** Not always.

Sometimes I get a code or a short explanation, and I have to figure out what it means.

I may need to call the sponsor or program representative. They are usually helpful, but it takes time, and sometimes I have to leave a message and wait for a response.

**Interviewer:** What are the main sources of income for your program?

**Deborah:** Most of it comes from parent tuition and childcare subsidies.

The food program helps cover some of the cost of meals and snacks. I have also applied for grants in the past, especially during the pandemic and for safety improvements.

The grants are helpful, but the application process can be difficult.

**Interviewer:** What makes grant applications difficult?

**Deborah:** Finding the grant is the first problem.

Information is spread across websites, emails, Facebook groups, and provider associations. Sometimes I hear about an opportunity after the deadline has already passed.

Then there is the application. They may ask for receipts, enrollment numbers, tax information, a written explanation, and proof that the money will be used for a specific purpose.

I understand that they need accountability, but every program asks for the information in a different way.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever decided not to apply for funding because of the paperwork?

**Deborah:** Yes.

If the grant is only for a few hundred dollars and the application looks like it will take several hours, I may decide it is not worth it.

That is unfortunate because small programs need the money. We just do not have an administrative department to handle the application.

I am the teacher, the cook, the cleaner, the bookkeeper, and the director.

**Interviewer:** What expenses are hardest to plan for?

**Deborah:** Repairs and major equipment.

If the refrigerator breaks, I cannot wait several weeks to replace it. If something happens to the heating system or a safety gate, I need to fix it immediately.

Food costs are also unpredictable. Reimbursement helps, but it does not always cover the full cost.

Insurance, licensing fees, training, toys, cleaning supplies, and increased utility costs all add up too.

**Interviewer:** How do you keep track of licensing requirements?

**Deborah:** I use a calendar, a binder, and reminders on my phone.

I track CPR certification, first aid, required training, medical forms, emergency contacts, inspections, and enrollment paperwork.

The difficult part is that every child has different expiration dates. Staff training dates are different too.

It is easy to miss something unless you check constantly.

**Interviewer:** What happens when a child’s paperwork expires?

**Deborah:** I have to contact the parent and ask for an updated form.

Sometimes I ask several times. Parents are busy, and they may not understand that I am required to have the document.

I do not want to punish the child because the parent has not returned a form. At the same time, I am responsible if an inspector asks for it.

**Interviewer:** Have you experienced an audit or monitoring visit?

**Deborah:** Yes. I have had licensing inspections and food program monitoring visits.

Some are scheduled, and some are unannounced.

The reviewer usually looks at attendance, meal records, enrollment documents, menus, health and safety items, and training records.

**Interviewer:** How do you prepare?

**Deborah:** I try to stay prepared all the time, but realistically, I review everything before a scheduled visit.

I check that forms are current, records match, and everything is organized.

The stressful part is knowing that a small mistake can become a finding, even if the children are safe and receiving good care.

**Interviewer:** Can you describe a time when records were difficult to locate?

**Deborah:** During one review, I was asked for an older enrollment form for a child who was no longer attending.

I knew I had it, but it was in a box with older records. It took me about 20 minutes to find it.

That does not sound long, but it feels very long when someone is sitting at your kitchen table waiting.

After that, I reorganized everything by year and child.

**Interviewer:** How do audits and reviews make you feel?

**Deborah:** Nervous.

I am confident in the care I provide. I know the children are safe, fed, and cared for.

But paperwork is different. You can do the actual work correctly and still get in trouble because you missed a signature or entered something in the wrong place.

It sometimes feels like the paperwork is treated as more important than the quality of care.

**Interviewer:** What software do you currently use?

**Deborah:** I use a childcare management application for attendance, parent communication, and some billing.

I use a separate system for food program reporting. I use spreadsheets for expenses and taxes.

Then I still have paper forms for licensing and parent signatures.

**Interviewer:** What does your childcare application do well?

**Deborah:** Parent communication is probably the best part.

I can send messages, pictures, reminders, and announcements. Parents like having everything in one place.

It is also helpful for sending invoices and tracking payments.

**Interviewer:** What does it do poorly?

**Deborah:** It tries to do too many things, so some parts are complicated.

There are screens I rarely use, and sometimes it takes too many taps to record a simple activity.

It also does not connect to the food program system or the subsidy system. I still have to enter information again.

**Interviewer:** How well does it work on a phone?

**Deborah:** It works, but I would not say it is always easy.

Some buttons are small. Some forms are long. If I am holding a baby or standing outside, it is not practical.

The system seems like it was designed for someone sitting at a desk. Home childcare providers are rarely sitting at a desk.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever stopped using a childcare application?

**Deborah:** Yes. I tried another one a few years ago.

It had a lot of features, but it took too long to set up and was confusing for parents. I was spending more time managing the application than it saved me.

After a few months, I cancelled it.

**Interviewer:** What would make a system worth using?

**Deborah:** It has to save time.

It should not ask me to enter information that already exists somewhere else. Attendance, meals, billing, and reporting should work together.

It also needs to be simple enough to use while I am caring for children.

I would want large buttons, quick entries, and the ability to correct mistakes without going through several screens.

**Interviewer:** Would voice entry be useful?

**Deborah:** Possibly, especially during busy times.

It would be helpful to say something like, “Emma ate lunch,” or “Noah went down for a nap at 12:30.”

But it would need to be accurate. I would also want to review the information before it became part of an official report.

Privacy would matter too. I would not want the system recording everything happening in the room.

**Interviewer:** Which administrative tasks take the most time away from caring for children?

**Deborah:** Repeating information is the biggest waste of time.

Attendance is entered in one place. Meals are entered somewhere else. Billing is another system. Licensing documents are stored somewhere else.

None of the systems talk to each other.

Tracking down missing parent forms also takes a lot of time.

**Interviewer:** When do you usually complete paperwork?

**Deborah:** Early in the morning, during nap time, or after the children leave.

Nap time is not always reliable because not every child sleeps.

A lot of the work happens at night or on weekends. That is time I would rather spend with my family or resting.

**Interviewer:** How often do you feel behind?

**Deborah:** Fairly often.

I am usually caught up on the things that affect the children immediately. The paperwork that is due later gets pushed back.

Then suddenly the deadline is close, and I have to rush.

**Interviewer:** What mistakes worry you the most?

**Deborah:** Anything that could affect my license or payment.

I worry about missing an attendance entry, forgetting an expiration date, or submitting a claim incorrectly.

I also worry about making a mistake that looks dishonest even though it was accidental.

**Interviewer:** Does the administrative workload affect your willingness to continue providing childcare?

**Deborah:** Sometimes.

I love working with the children. That is not the part that makes me think about stopping.

It is the paperwork, the long hours, the financial uncertainty, and feeling like there is always another requirement.

A lot of experienced providers are leaving. New providers see how much is involved and decide not to start.

**Interviewer:** What do people outside home-based childcare often misunderstand?

**Deborah:** They think we are babysitting.

They do not understand that we run a small business while also caring for children.

We have licensing rules, taxes, insurance, food regulations, parent contracts, training requirements, inspections, and reporting.

We do all of that without office staff.

**Interviewer:** Imagine the administrative side of your program worked exactly the way you wanted. What would be different?

**Deborah:** I would record information once.

If a child checked in, the system would know the child was present for breakfast or lunch. I would only need to confirm what was served.

Parent billing, subsidy attendance, and meal reporting would use the same attendance information.

The system would remind me before forms or certifications expired.

It would also show me what needs attention without making me search through several menus.

**Interviewer:** What would you want to see at the beginning of the day?

**Deborah:** I would want to see which children are expected, who is absent, whether anyone has a schedule change, and whether there are any missing forms or parent messages.

I would also want to see meal plans and allergy reminders.

**Interviewer:** What would you want to see at the end of the day?

**Deborah:** I would want a simple summary.

Who attended, whether all meals and activities were recorded, which parents need updates, and whether anything is incomplete.

I would want to fix missing information before I forget what happened.

**Interviewer:** What would help you feel ready for an audit?

**Deborah:** A system that could show me that my records are complete.

I would want to choose a date range and generate attendance, meals, enrollment information, and other required documents.

I should not have to search through multiple applications and binders.

**Interviewer:** What would make funding and reimbursement more predictable?

**Deborah:** Clear information about what was submitted, what was approved, and when I will be paid.

If there is a problem, I would want the system to tell me exactly what needs to be corrected.

I would also want alerts when a child’s authorization is about to expire or when a family’s status changes.

**Interviewer:** What would make you distrust a childcare management system?

**Deborah:** Incorrect records.

If it changed attendance, lost information, or submitted something without letting me review it, I would stop using it.

I would also be concerned if I did not know who could access the children’s information.

Support matters too. If something affects my payment, I need to be able to reach a real person.

**Interviewer:** Of everything we discussed, what is the biggest challenge?

**Deborah:** The biggest challenge is that the systems are disconnected.

Each program wants similar information, but they all want it entered separately.

That creates extra work and more opportunities for mistakes.

**Interviewer:** Which problem creates the greatest financial risk?

**Deborah:** Subsidy and reimbursement errors.

If I make a mistake with attendance or a claim, I may not get paid for care I already provided.

A large childcare center may be able to absorb a delay. A home provider may not be able to.

**Interviewer:** Which problem creates the most stress?

**Deborah:** Compliance.

There is always a fear that I missed something. Even when I think I am caught up, I wonder whether there is a form, rule, or deadline I forgot about.

**Interviewer:** If you could solve one problem first, what would it be?

**Deborah:** I would create one reliable attendance record that could be used for everything else.

Attendance affects parent billing, subsidy payments, meal reimbursement, and licensing.

If that information was accurate and shared correctly, it would solve a lot of other problems.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything important that I did not ask?

**Deborah:** I would add that providers need tools designed for our environment.

We are not offices. We are homes filled with children.

A tool can have every feature in the world, but if it takes too long to use during the day, providers will go back to paper.

The technology has to fit the work. The work should not have to change to fit the technology.

**Interviewer:** That is a helpful distinction. Thank you for sharing your experience, Deborah.

**Deborah:** You are welcome. I am glad people are looking at this because the administrative side of childcare does not get enough attention.
