- **Participant:** Angie
- **Role:** New York home-based childcare provider
- **Primary topics:** Constant supervision, delayed documentation, memory-based recordkeeping, exhaustion, compliance, and reporting accuracy

**Interviewer:** Thank you for speaking with me today, Angie. I am researching the challenges home-based childcare providers face when managing daily care, documentation, reporting, and funding.

I am especially interested in how state supervision requirements affect your ability to complete records during the day.

Can you start by telling me about your childcare program?

**Angie:** I run a licensed childcare program from my home in New York.

I have been doing this for about 11 years. I usually care for six children during the day. Most of them are toddlers and preschoolers, although I sometimes have school-age children before or after school.

I manage most of the program by myself.

**Interviewer:** Do you have an assistant?

**Angie:** I have someone who helps a few hours a week, but not every day.

Most days, I am the only adult responsible for the children.

That means I am supervising, preparing meals, cleaning, helping with the bathroom, changing diapers, leading activities, managing behavior, and communicating with parents.

**Interviewer:** What does a typical day look like?

**Angie:** The first child usually arrives around 7:00 in the morning.

The morning is very busy because children arrive at different times. I talk with parents, help children transition, serve breakfast, and make sure everyone is settled.

Then we have activities, free play, outside time, lunch, naps, snacks, and pickups.

There is never really a quiet time.

Even when some children are sleeping, not all of them sleep. I still need to monitor the room and respond if someone wakes up or needs help.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned that New York requires constant monitoring of the children. How does that affect your day?

**Angie:** It affects everything.

I am responsible for knowing where the children are and what they are doing at all times.

I cannot sit at a desk in another room and complete paperwork. I cannot focus on a phone or computer for several minutes while the children are playing.

Even if the children appear calm, something can happen very quickly.

A child can climb on something, push another child, put something in their mouth, or need help in the bathroom.

**Interviewer:** Are you able to document activities while they are happening?

**Angie:** Not in the way the systems expect me to.

I may be able to make a quick checkmark on paper or write one word, but I cannot complete detailed documentation throughout the day.

Some applications expect you to enter the exact time a child ate, slept, used the bathroom, or participated in an activity.

That sounds simple until you are supervising six children.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me an example?

**Angie:** Lunch is a good example.

I may have one child refusing to eat, another asking for more milk, another dropping food on the floor, and another child who needs help using a spoon.

I am watching for choking, allergies, spills, and behavior problems.

I cannot stop after each child eats and enter the information into an application.

My attention needs to be on the children.

**Interviewer:** What do you do instead?

**Angie:** I try to remember.

Sometimes I make quick notes on a paper near the kitchen. I might write a child’s initials or a short reminder.

But many times I do not have a free hand.

I tell myself I will document it later.

**Interviewer:** When does later usually happen?

**Angie:** At night.

After the last child leaves, I clean the childcare area, wash dishes, sanitize toys, prepare supplies, and sometimes respond to parent messages.

Then I make dinner and take care of my own household.

I usually start the official documentation around 8:00 or 9:00 at night.

Sometimes I am still working after 11:00.

**Interviewer:** Is that every night?

**Angie:** Almost every night.

There may be a day when I have less to enter, but there is always something.

Attendance, meals, naps, diaper changes, activities, parent communication, incident notes, or food program records.

I rarely feel completely caught up.

**Interviewer:** How do you reconstruct the day?

**Angie:** I go through each child one at a time.

I think about when they arrived, what they ate, whether they slept, and what activities we did.

I look at any paper notes I made.

Sometimes I look at text messages from parents or photos I took during the day.

I may also look at the clock on a photo to estimate when an activity happened.

**Interviewer:** How accurate do you feel those records are?

**Angie:** I do my best, but I know they are not always exact.

I may remember that a child started a nap around 12:30, but I may not remember whether it was 12:25 or 12:40.

I may remember that everyone ate snack, but I may have trouble remembering whether one child left before snack that day.

By nighttime, many details blend together.

**Interviewer:** Does that worry you?

**Angie:** Yes, a lot.

The records are treated like they were entered at the exact moment something happened.

But many providers are entering them hours later.

We are expected to supervise constantly and document constantly. Those two expectations do not work together when one person is doing both jobs.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever made a documentation mistake because you were relying on memory?

**Angie:** Yes.

I once recorded a child as eating an afternoon snack because I remembered serving snack to the group.

Then I realized the child had been picked up early that day.

The attendance record and meal record did not match.

I corrected it, but it made me worry about how many smaller mistakes I may not notice.

**Interviewer:** What happens when records do not match?

**Angie:** It can affect reimbursement.

It can also create questions during a review or inspection.

If attendance says a child was not present, but the meal record says the child ate, it looks like I am claiming something incorrectly.

It may be an honest memory mistake, but the system does not know that.

**Interviewer:** Does that make you concerned that an accidental mistake could look intentional?

**Angie:** Yes.

That is one of my biggest fears.

I would never intentionally submit false information.

But when you enter records late at night while exhausted, it is possible to click the wrong child or choose the wrong time.

A reviewer may only see the inconsistency. They do not see the 11-hour day that happened before I entered it.

**Interviewer:** What kinds of information are you required to document?

**Angie:** Daily attendance, meals, snacks, naps, diaper changes, bathroom use, incidents, medication, and sometimes activities.

I also maintain enrollment records, emergency contacts, health forms, training records, and licensing documents.

For the food program, I need accurate meal and attendance information.

For childcare assistance, attendance may affect payment.

**Interviewer:** Which records are most difficult to complete from memory?

**Angie:** Exact times are the hardest.

I usually remember whether something happened. I remember that a child slept or ate.

But remembering the exact start and end time is difficult.

The more interruptions there are during the day, the harder it becomes.

**Interviewer:** What kinds of interruptions are common?

**Angie:** Almost everything is an interruption.

A child needs the bathroom. Someone falls. A parent calls. A child is crying. Someone spills a drink. A delivery arrives. A licensing agency sends an email. Two children argue over a toy.

The work is built around interruptions.

That is normal in childcare, but the documentation systems often assume you can stop and focus.

**Interviewer:** Do you feel the systems are designed for home-based providers?

**Angie:** No.

They feel like they were designed for childcare centers with multiple staff members.

In a center, one teacher may supervise while another person handles paperwork.

A home provider may be the teacher, director, cook, cleaner, and administrator at the same time.

The system does not account for that.

**Interviewer:** Have you tried documenting on your phone during the day?

**Angie:** Yes, but it is still difficult.

Opening the phone takes my attention away from the room.

Then I have to find the right child, choose the activity, enter the time, and save it.

If I am interrupted before I finish, I may forget whether the information saved.

Small buttons and long menus make it worse.

**Interviewer:** Does using a phone create any other concerns?

**Angie:** Parents or inspectors may see you looking at a phone and assume you are distracted.

They do not know whether you are documenting a child’s meal or reading a personal message.

I do not want to appear like I am not paying attention.

**Interviewer:** Do you use paper notes instead?

**Angie:** Sometimes.

Paper is faster, but it creates another step.

I still have to enter the information into the official system later.

Paper can also get wet, misplaced, or mixed up.

If I write too quickly, I may not understand my own note at night.

**Interviewer:** Can you describe your nighttime documentation process?

**Angie:** I sit down with my paper notes, attendance sheet, phone, and computer.

I open the childcare application and the food program system.

Then I go child by child and try to recreate the day.

I compare arrival and departure times. I enter meals and snacks. I enter naps and other activities.

If something does not make sense, I stop and think about what happened.

Sometimes I send a parent a message to confirm a pickup time.

**Interviewer:** How long does this usually take?

**Angie:** On a good night, maybe an hour.

On a difficult night, two or three hours.

If I am behind from the previous day, it can take longer.

**Interviewer:** What makes a night difficult?

**Angie:** If there were schedule changes, early pickups, absences, incidents, or several parent messages, it takes longer.

It is also harder if I am exhausted.

Some nights I stare at the screen and cannot remember what happened.

I know I served lunch, but I have to think through where each child was sitting and who was present.

**Interviewer:** What do you do when you cannot remember?

**Angie:** I use the best information I have.

I check messages, photos, attendance sheets, and notes.

If I still cannot remember, I enter what I believe is correct.

That is uncomfortable because I want the record to be accurate.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever left something undocumented because you could not remember?

**Angie:** Yes.

If I am not confident that something happened, I would rather leave it blank than enter something incorrect.

But leaving it blank can also create a problem.

It may mean I cannot claim reimbursement, or it may look like I failed to complete the record.

**Interviewer:** It sounds like there is risk either way.

**Angie:** Exactly.

If I enter something based on memory, it may be wrong.

If I leave it blank, the record is incomplete.

There is no good option when the information was not captured during the day.

**Interviewer:** How does staying up late affect you?

**Angie:** I am tired all the time.

I usually wake up early to prepare before the first child arrives.

If I stay up until 11:00 or midnight documenting, I do not get enough sleep.

Then I start the next childcare day already tired.

**Interviewer:** Does the fatigue affect your work?

**Angie:** Yes.

I have less patience and less energy.

I still take care of the children, but it is harder.

I may also be more likely to make documentation mistakes the next night because I am even more tired.

It becomes a cycle.

**Interviewer:** Does it affect your personal life?

**Angie:** Very much.

My evenings are not really mine.

My family knows that after dinner I usually have to work.

Sometimes they are watching television or relaxing, and I am sitting with paperwork.

Even when I go to bed, I may be thinking about whether I forgot something.

**Interviewer:** Have you considered reducing the number of children in your care?

**Angie:** Yes.

Fewer children would mean fewer records and less stress.

But it would also mean less income.

My expenses would not decrease enough to make that easy.

**Interviewer:** Have you considered hiring help?

**Angie:** I would like to, but it is expensive.

I would need someone qualified and reliable.

The extra income from having more children might go directly toward paying that person.

It would help with supervision, but it might not improve the business financially.

**Interviewer:** Does the administrative workload make you consider leaving childcare?

**Angie:** Sometimes.

I enjoy caring for the children.

The paperwork is what makes me question whether I can continue.

I do not want to spend every night reconstructing my day.

**Interviewer:** What do people misunderstand about constant supervision?

**Angie:** They think supervision means being in the same room.

It is more than that.

I have to actively watch, listen, anticipate problems, and respond quickly.

I cannot give full attention to a form while also giving full attention to six young children.

When people say, “Just enter it when it happens,” they do not understand the reality.

**Interviewer:** Do you think the regulations and documentation requirements contradict each other?

**Angie:** Yes.

I understand why both exist.

Children need supervision, and records need to be accurate.

But the state needs to recognize that one provider cannot do both tasks continuously at the same time.

The requirements should be designed around how the work actually happens.

**Interviewer:** What kind of technology might help?

**Angie:** It would need to let me document without looking away from the children for long.

Voice entry might help.

I could press one button and say, “Everyone started lunch at 11:45,” or “Liam went down for a nap at 12:20.”

**Interviewer:** Would you trust voice documentation?

**Angie:** I would want to review it later.

The system could make a quick draft during the day and let me approve it at night.

That would be much better than recreating the whole day from memory.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the original voice recording saved?

**Angie:** Possibly for a short period.

It could help prove what I said if the transcription was wrong.

But it would need strong privacy controls.

I would not want the system recording the children all day.

It should only activate when I choose to record something.

**Interviewer:** What else could help?

**Angie:** Group entries would help.

If all six children eat lunch at the same time, I should be able to record the group and then remove anyone who was absent.

I should not have to enter the same meal six times.

**Interviewer:** What about automatic timestamps?

**Angie:** That would be useful.

If I press a meal button, the system should record the current time automatically.

I should not have to select the time from a menu.

The fewer steps, the more likely I can use it during the day.

**Interviewer:** Would a wearable device help?

**Angie:** Maybe.

A watch or simple button might be easier than using a phone.

I could tap it and record a quick voice note.

But it would have to be reliable and very simple.

I would not want another complicated device to manage.

**Interviewer:** What would an end-of-day review look like?

**Angie:** I would want one screen showing the whole day.

It should tell me what was recorded and what is missing.

For example, it might say that one child has no departure time or that lunch was not confirmed for two children.

I could review everything in 10 or 15 minutes instead of rebuilding the entire day.

**Interviewer:** How would that change your evenings?

**Angie:** I might be able to finish before dinner.

Even finishing by 7:00 would make a huge difference.

I could spend time with my family and go to bed without worrying.

**Interviewer:** What would make the system feel trustworthy?

**Angie:** It should never submit official records without my approval.

It should show when the information was recorded and when it was edited.

If I correct something, it should keep a clear history.

I would also want to know that my records are backed up.

**Interviewer:** Would an audit trail make you feel more protected?

**Angie:** Yes.

If I enter a note during the day and correct the official time later, the system should show that clearly.

That would demonstrate that I was not trying to hide anything.

It would show how the record was created.

**Interviewer:** What would help during inspections or audits?

**Angie:** I would want the system to check for conflicts.

If attendance and meal records do not match, it should warn me before I submit anything.

If a child is marked absent, it should not let me accidentally claim a meal without asking me to confirm it.

**Interviewer:** How do reporting and documentation affect funding?

**Angie:** If the record is missing or incorrect, I may lose reimbursement.

The food was purchased and served. The care was provided. But payment depends on the documentation.

That makes accurate records financially important, even when the amount for one meal seems small.

**Interviewer:** What is the biggest challenge you face?

**Angie:** The biggest challenge is that I cannot safely document in detail while supervising the children.

Everything else comes from that.

Because I cannot document during the day, I have to rely on memory at night.

Because I rely on memory, I worry about mistakes.

Because I worry about mistakes, the work takes even longer.

**Interviewer:** Which problem causes the most stress?

**Angie:** Knowing that I may be judged by records created when I was exhausted.

The records do not show the quality of care I gave during the day.

They show how well I could remember the day at 10:00 at night.

**Interviewer:** Which problem creates the greatest financial risk?

**Angie:** Incorrect attendance and meal records.

Those can affect subsidy payments and food reimbursement.

They can also create concerns during a review.

**Interviewer:** What would you solve first?

**Angie:** I would create a way to capture a quick, reliable record without taking my attention away from the children.

It could be a voice command, a large button, or a simple group action.

The important thing is capturing the information when it happens.

Then I could review it later instead of trying to remember everything.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything else you would like people to understand?

**Angie:** Providers are not delaying documentation because we are careless.

We delay it because supervising the children has to come first.

The system asks us to choose between watching the children and completing the record.

We choose the children, and then we pay for that choice with our evenings and our sleep.

**Interviewer:** Thank you, Angie. That is a clear description of the conflict between supervision and documentation.

**Angie:** Thank you. I hope people understand that the solution is not telling providers to try harder.

The solution is creating requirements and tools that match the reality of the job.
